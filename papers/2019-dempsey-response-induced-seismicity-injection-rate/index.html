Response of Induced Seismicity to Injection Rate Reduction: Models of Delay, Decay, Quiescence, Recovery, and Oklahoma

In brief

We modelled how injection-triggered earthquakes respond to a cut in injection rate. The earthquake rate can fall by more than the cut and stop for a time before it partly recovers. For Western Oklahoma, our forecasts suggest the 2016 volume limit is inadequate.

  • When faults need a minimum pressure rise before they slip, cutting injection reduces the earthquake rate by more than the size of the cut.
  • After a cut, earthquakes can stop for a period and then partly return, reaching up to 25% of the pre-cut rate in the paper's examples.
  • Calibrated to Western Oklahoma, our model reproduced the onset of earthquakes in early 2014, the peak in 2016, and the decline to September 2018.
  • In our forecasts at the September 2018 rate, the chance of magnitude 5 or larger before 2025 was 14 to 46%, and 81 to 90% at the mandated maximum.
  • Halving injection to 1.5 million cubic metres a month returned the earthquake rate to background levels between mid-2019 and mid-2021 in our forecasts.

The problem

Pumping fluid underground can trigger earthquakes (Ellsworth, 2013). In 2016, the Oklahoma Corporation Commission ordered a 40% cut in wastewater volumes injected into two regions with many earthquakes (Langenbruch & Zoback, 2016). However, at Paradox Valley, Colorado, a 35% injection cut reduced earthquakes but did not stop them (Ake et al., 2005).

Our goal was to determine what should be expected after an injection cut, and to test whether the Oklahoma limit protects against earthquakes of magnitude 5 or larger.

What we did

We built models of how fluid pressure spreads from injection wells, from a single well in a thin, wide aquifer to injection spread over a circle above faulted basement rock. In the model, faults fail only once the pressure has risen by a critical amount, the critical triggering pressure. Above that threshold, the earthquake rate at a location is proportional to how fast the pressure there is rising. If pressure falls, earthquakes stop at that location and restart only when pressure exceeds its previous maximum.

For Western Oklahoma, we spread the injected water evenly over a circle 50 km in radius, in a 500 m thick layer representing the Arbuckle formation. Pressure then diffuses down faults into the basement, where most earthquakes occur at about 4.5 km depth (Schoenball & Ellsworth, 2017). We estimated four unknowns, including the critical triggering pressure, by comparing 4,680 model runs with the 914 earthquakes of magnitude 3 or larger recorded in the region before September 2018.

What we found

When faults are already at the point of failure (a critical pressure of zero), the earthquake rate eventually falls in proportion to the cut. When faults need some pressure rise to fail, the rate falls by more than the cut. This is because pressure near the well declines for a time after the cut. Earthquakes there stop until it rises again above its earlier maximum. If the threshold is high enough, earthquakes stop everywhere for a period. They then return as pressure rebuilds, towards a new rate below the pre-cut rate. In the paper's examples, this recovery reached up to 25% of the pre-cut rate. However, a falling earthquake rate soon after a cut does not reliably indicate the long-term earthquake rate.

The widget uses our reconstruction of the paper's single-well model. Its critical pressure is dimensionless. Larger values represent faults further from failure. Time is counted in multiples of the injection period before the cut. With a 40% cut and a critical pressure of 1.3, earthquakes stop from 0.16 to 0.59 injection periods after the cut. They then recover towards 24% of the pre-cut rate. At some other settings, the recovery exceeds the paper's 25%.

In Western Oklahoma, our model reproduced the onset of earthquakes in early 2014, the peak in 2016, and the decline to September 2018. We estimated an Arbuckle diffusivity of 44 to 277 m²/s, enough for pressure to spread about 40 to 90 km in a year. This is an order of magnitude or more higher than values used in earlier studies (e.g., Goebel et al., 2017). We estimated a critical triggering pressure of 0.021 to 0.077 MPa, the extra pressure at the bottom of 2 to 8 m of water, and a pressure rise at 4.5 km depth of up to 0.190 MPa by September 2018. Once the threshold has been passed, the earthquake rate is sensitive to changes in injection. In our model, the decline results from operators cutting injection by more than the mandate required.

In our forecasts, if injection holds at its September 2018 rate of 3.0 million m³ a month (about 40 Olympic swimming pools a day), the earthquake rate dips to a minimum in 2021 and then rises to 1.4 to 4.1 magnitude 3 events a month by 2025. The chance of a magnitude 5 or larger earthquake before 2025 is 14 to 46%. At the mandated maximum of 4.0 million m³ a month, that chance is 81 to 90%. Halving injection to 1.5 million m³ a month returns the earthquake rate to background levels between mid-2019 and mid-2021, although the chance of magnitude 5 could still be as high as 10%.

For the same three injection rates, Langenbruch and Zoback (2016) forecast the same 32% chance of magnitude 5. We argue that the mandate, in its present form, is inadequate. Operators have been injecting below the limit voluntarily, and no regulation prevents them from returning to it.

At the diffusivities we estimated, pressure increases from neighbouring wells overlap. Earthquakes then respond to all the wells together. Regulating wells one at a time, for example with a traffic light system, may be both unfair and ineffective. Cutting one well may have no long-term effect on earthquakes triggered by a regional rise in pressure.

Limitations

Our models assume uniform rock and a single critical pressure. They treat hundreds of wells as one evenly spread source, without individual wells, known faults, or poroelastic effects. As a result, they describe the average response of many faults. Omitting poroelastic effects may have inflated our estimate of Arbuckle diffusivity. Alternatively, earlier studies may have underestimated it.

Our model and that of Langenbruch and Zoback (2016) both fit past earthquakes, yet their forecasts disagree. This is because theirs uses an injection-rate threshold and ours a pressure threshold. Agreement with past data alone does not distinguish between them. We suggest that the physics and assumptions of each model also need critical assessment.

Glossary

An aquifer is a permeable rock layer that fluid can flow through.

The Arbuckle formation is the aquifer that receives most of the wastewater injected in Oklahoma.

Basement is the crystalline rock beneath the Arbuckle formation, where most of the Oklahoma earthquakes occur.

The critical triggering pressure is the pressure rise a fault needs before it slips.

Diffusivity measures how quickly a pressure change spreads through rock. It rises with permeability.

Permeability measures how easily fluid flows through rock.

Poroelastic effects are changes in rock stress caused by fluid pressure changes some distance away.

A traffic light system reduces or stops injection at a well when nearby earthquakes exceed set levels.

A dimensionless pressure has no units. It is the pressure rise divided by a reference pressure set by the injection rate and how easily the well takes fluid.

Magnitude is a logarithmic measure of earthquake size.

The background rate is the rate of earthquakes expected without injection.

Abstract

When injection‐induced seismicity poses a risk to communities, it is common to reduce the injection rate or halt operations. This applies both to individual wells and well clusters, such as those within the Area of Interest for Triggered Seismicity in Western Oklahoma, where in 2016 a 40% volume reduction mandate was imposed by the state regulator. Here we quantify how induced seismicity responds to an injection reduction. We introduce models of pressure diffusion in idealized geometries coupled to steady state pressurization and rate‐state models of earthquake triggering. We find that the delay in seismicity onset and then postreduction behavior—decay, sometimes quiescence, and recovery of the seismicity rate—depend on the critical triggering pressure and on diffusion and rate‐state parameters. We have adapted our model to replicate the timing of onset, peak, and recent pace of decline of seismicity triggered by wastewater injection in Western Oklahoma. Our analysis implies that diffusivity in the Arbuckle formation is high (between 44 and 277 m2/s). The critical triggering pressure is inferred to be between 0.021 and 0.077 MPa, and fluid overpressure at 4.5‐km depth in the basement is estimated to have increased by as much as 0.190 MPa. We simulate future seismicity out to 2025 for three scenarios. Fixing the 2018 injection rate, already less than the limit imposed by the state regulator, we find a high likelihood of further M ≥ 5 earthquakes. This suggests that the volume reduction mandate in Western Oklahoma is, at present levels, inadequate.
