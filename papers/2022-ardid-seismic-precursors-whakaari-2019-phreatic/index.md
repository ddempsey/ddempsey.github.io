## In brief

We searched seismic tremor recorded before 18 eruptions at six volcanoes for a shared precursor. A rise in the ratio of medium- to high-frequency tremor recurred before steam-driven eruptions at Whakaari and, less clearly, Ruapehu and Tongariro. At Whakaari in 2019, we interpret it as shallow sealing and pressurisation.

- We analysed a combined 50 years of seismic tremor from six volcanoes in New Zealand, Alaska and Kamchatka, covering the weeks before 18 eruptions.
- The 48-hour median of the medium- to high-frequency tremor ratio rose to a peak about 2 to 4 days before eruptions at several volcanoes.
- Before each of four other Whakaari eruptions, this pattern resembled the 2019 pattern more closely than 90% of four-week periods without eruptions.
- The pattern was partly transferable to steam-driven eruptions at Ruapehu and Tongariro. For magmatic eruptions in Alaska and Kamchatka, results were not clearly different from a random benchmark.
- We interpret the 2019 Whakaari signal as a hydrothermal seal forming and shallow groundwater pressurising in the final week, with the seal failing over the last 16 hours.

## The problem

Steam-driven (phreatic) eruptions usually occur with little warning. In 2014 an
eruption at Mt Ontake in Japan killed 60 people (Yamaoka et al., 2016). In 2019
an eruption at Whakaari (White Island) in New Zealand killed 22 (Dempsey et al.,
2020). Even with real-time monitoring, recognising an imminent eruption at
Whakaari remains difficult. This is partly because pressurisation and eruption
triggering occur within a few tens to hundreds of metres of the surface
(Montanaro et al., 2016; Jolly et al., 2020).

Our goal was to find a seismic precursor that (1) recurs before multiple
eruptions, (2) is rare when the volcano is not erupting, and (3) appears at more
than one volcano.

## What we did

We analysed volcanic tremor recorded before 18 eruptions at six volcanoes:
Whakaari, Ruapehu and Tongariro in New Zealand, Veniaminof and Pavlof in Alaska,
and Bezymianny in Kamchatka, Russia. We processed a combined 50 years of data,
from one seismometer near the vent of each volcano.

We measured tremor in three frequency bands, 2 to 5, 4.5 to 8 and 8 to 16 hertz
(vibrations per second). Our main quantity is the displacement seismic amplitude
ratio (DSAR), the ratio of ground displacement in the medium band to that in the
high band. High-frequency shaking is attenuated more strongly as it travels up
from below the surface. A change in the ratio may therefore reflect a change in
the shallow rock, such as sealing (Caudron et al., 2019).

We normalised the records, divided them into overlapping 48-hour windows, and
computed 524 features for each window of the three bands and DSAR, about 2000 in
all. For each feature, we correlated the four weeks before each eruption with
those before every other eruption, and shortlisted the features with the highest
average correlation.

To test each candidate, we correlated the four weeks before one eruption, the
template, with every four-week segment of the record. A Kolmogorov–Smirnov test
then compared correlations before eruptions with those during repose. We
repeated this with each eruption as the template, shifted back at random by up
to 7 days to allow for differences in timing, and compared the results with a
benchmark built from templates taken at random from repose.

## What we found

Two features passed these tests. The first is the nDSAR median, the median of
normalised DSAR over 48 hours. Before several eruptions it rose steadily to a
peak about 2 to 4 days beforehand. The second feature, nDSAR rate variance,
measures how much DSAR changes between consecutive values. It was persistently
high 5 to 10 days before six major eruptions, one at each volcano.

With Whakaari 2019 as the template, the nDSAR median before each of the other
four Whakaari eruptions correlated more closely than 90% of four-week periods
from more than 40 years without eruptions. The p-value was 0.00015. This
suggests that the correlations before eruptions come from a different
distribution from those during repose. Eruptions at Ruapehu, Tongariro and
Veniaminof also ranked highly, with larger p-values of 0.017, 0.066 and 0.072. A
third candidate, a 75-minute oscillation in high-frequency tremor, was strong
before six eruptions. However, it also appeared to occur frequently during
repose. We rejected it as spurious.

In repeated tests, both features distinguished the five Whakaari eruptions from
the benchmark. They did so less clearly when steam-driven eruptions at Ruapehu
and Tongariro were added. When Whakaari eruptions were pooled with magmatic
eruptions at Veniaminof, Pavlof and Bezymianny, the results were not obviously
different from the benchmark. We suggest that eruption style limits
transferability. This would be expected if the precursor records a physical
process specific to steam-driven eruptions.

With surface observations, we identified five phases before the 2019 Whakaari
eruption. We interpret sustained tremor from 10 to 23 November as magma
interacting with groundwater (phase 1). From 23 November to 2 December, day-long
tremor cycles accompanied frequent gas emissions and water jetting (phase 2). We
interpret each cycle as pressure building below a weak seal until the seal
failed.

After 3 December, medium-frequency tremor rose steadily, high-frequency tremor
did not, and the nDSAR median increased. No sulfur dioxide emissions or unusual
surface activity were reported. We hypothesise that this rise records a stronger
seal forming and the groundwater beneath it pressurising (phase 3). This is
because a seal reduces fluid release at the surface and with it the
high-frequency tremor. Continued fluid entry below the seal strengthens the
medium-frequency tremor. In the final days the nDSAR median declined. This could
indicate that the groundwater beneath the seal had reached an equilibrium
pressure with the fluid supply from depth (phase 4). About 16 hours before the
eruption, tremor increased in all bands (phase 5). We interpret this as cracking
of the seal that eventually allowed the pressurised fluid to escape explosively.

The 16-hour burst was recognised as a precursor in earlier work (Dempsey et al.,
2020). Under this interpretation, we show that pressurisation can be recognised
much earlier, in the week before the eruption.

## Limitations

Our analysis is retrospective. The correlation method is also sensitive to
differences in the timing and duration of a pattern. The random shifts correct
for timing only in part. Other methods may find patterns that ours missed. The
mechanism that formed the seal in 2019 is uncertain.

Real-time monitoring for these precursors may improve short-term eruption
warnings at some volcanoes. However, forecasting skill has to be demonstrated in
prospective experiments.

## Glossary

A **steam-driven (phreatic) eruption** is an explosion driven by hot,
pressurised fluid flashing to steam when it is suddenly released.

A **magmatic eruption** is one in which magma itself is erupted.

**Volcanic tremor** is continuous low-level shaking of the ground at an active
volcano.

The **displacement seismic amplitude ratio (DSAR)** is the ratio of tremor
displacement in the 4.5 to 8 Hz band to that in the 8 to 16 Hz band. The prefix
n means the values were normalised.

**Normalisation** rescales records from different volcanoes to a common scale so
that they can be compared.

A **hydrothermal seal** is a low-permeability layer that restricts the flow of
gas and water to the surface.

A **precursor** is a signal that occurs before eruptions and is rare at other
times, so that it could be used to warn of them.

A **feature** is a single number that summarises a segment of the tremor record,
such as its median.

A **correlation coefficient** measures how similar two curves are in shape,
equal to 1 for the same shape and near 0 for unrelated curves.

The **Kolmogorov–Smirnov test** checks whether two sets of values are likely to
come from the same distribution. A small p-value means they are unlikely to.

**Repose** is a period when a volcano is not erupting.

A **prospective experiment** tests forecasts that are issued before the outcome
is known.

## Abstract

Volcanic eruptions that occur without warning can be deadly in touristic and populated areas. Even with real-time geophysical monitoring, forecasting sudden eruptions is difficult, because their precursors are hard to recognize and can vary between volcanoes. Here, we describe a general seismic precursor signal for gas-driven eruptions, identified through correlation analysis of 18 well-recorded eruptions in New Zealand, Alaska, and Kamchatka. The precursor manifests in the displacement seismic amplitude ratio between medium (4.5–8 Hz) and high (8–16 Hz) frequency tremor bands, exhibiting a characteristic rise in the days prior to eruptions. We interpret this as formation of a hydrothermal seal that enables rapid pressurization of shallow groundwater. Applying this model to the 2019 eruption at Whakaari (New Zealand), we describe pressurization of the system in the week before the eruption, and cascading seal failure in the 16 h prior to the explosion. Real-time monitoring for this precursor may improve short-term eruption warning systems at certain volcanoes.
