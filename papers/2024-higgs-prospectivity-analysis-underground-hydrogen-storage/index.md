## The problem

In 2022, 87.1% of New Zealand's electricity came from renewable sources
(Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment, 2022). However, natural gas
and coal are still used to meet seasonal shortfalls in supply. One option is
to turn surplus renewable electricity into hydrogen by electrolysis, store it
underground while demand is low, and withdraw it as demand rises. Salt caverns
are currently the only proven stores for high-purity hydrogen, and New Zealand has no
salt deposits. Storage in porous rock, such as a depleted gas field, is
considered the most viable large-scale option for the country (Nicol et al.,
2022).

Using estimates from Yergin (2020) and Firstgas (2021), we assumed a 2050
hydrogen demand of 15 to 42 TWh a year. Ahuroa, the country's one underground
gas store, holds about a tenth of annual gas use. If the same share applies to
hydrogen in 2050, New Zealand would need 1.5 to 4.2 TWh of usable hydrogen
storage. That is 1 to 3% of the country's 2022 energy use of 150 TWh (MBIE
data, our arithmetic). These figures exclude cushion gas, the gas left in a
reservoir to maintain pressure.

## What we did

Taranaki Basin is New Zealand's only basin with commercial petroleum
production. We assessed 40 sites there: all significant past and present oil
and gas fields, one undeveloped gas discovery and one deep aquifer. We
estimated each site's hydrogen capacity from its recoverable gas and oil
reserves.

A panel of ten geoscientists chose 18 technical parameters in five groups:
site properties (trap structure, capacity, porosity and depth), injectivity
(how readily gas flows into and out of the rock), containment, reactions of
hydrogen with the rock and fluids, and data availability and quality. We
ranked the sites with a decision tree, a sequence of yes-or-no questions on
size, depth, permeability, proven oil or gas, reactivity, leakage and data.

We also ranked them with a weighted matrix. To enter it, a site needed 1 to 20
TWh of capacity, a reservoir top deeper than 1 km, and an average permeability
above 5 mD. Each qualifying site scored 1 (poorest) to 3 (best) on each
parameter, and each parameter had a weight from 1 to 5. The weighted,
normalised scores sum to a rank score between 0 and 1. The highest weights
were assigned to data quality, top-seal and fault-seal risk, and hydrocarbon
phase. We favoured gas reservoirs on the premise that a caprock proven to hold
natural gas is more likely to hold hydrogen. These weights are subject to our
own biases. We therefore tested the ranking with higher and lower weights on
reactivity parameters, and with higher weights on reservoir parameters.

## What we found

Both methods placed the same two sites first: Ahuroa, an onshore field now
used to store natural gas, and the Kaimiro reservoir of the offshore Maui A
field. Their matrix scores were 0.806 and 0.796. However, Ahuroa's position
depends partly on confidential data from its gas storage operation. The
neighbouring Tariki field has a broadly similar structure, reservoir and seal
but scored 0.756, mainly because its public data are poorer.

Miocene reservoirs ranked lowest. Typically, they are small, split into
separate pools, and more likely to hold oil than gas. They are also often
poorly lithified, and their fine-grained, clay-rich sands may degrade under
repeated injection and withdrawal. The deep aquifer scored lowest of all
(0.525), mainly because its containment is unproven and few of its data are
public.

The top ten scores spanned 0.725 to 0.806. Input from other experts could
change their order. When we lowered the weights on reactivity, Maui A moved to
first place, and Ahuroa led in the other three. Paleogene reservoirs, mostly
gas fields or gas caps above oil, filled the top ten in all four weighting

Our results suggest that developing one or two depleted fields would provide
enough storage for New Zealand by 2050. Together, the two leading sites have
an estimated maximum capacity of 23 TWh of hydrogen, including cushion gas. Of
this, 21.6 TWh is at Maui A (Table 6), above the 20 TWh entry limit for the
matrix.

At the cushion gas share of about 30 to 50% that we cite for depleted fields
(Ennis-King et al., 2021), the two sites would provide 11.5 to 16.1 TWh of
working gas (our arithmetic). Our estimates of the 2050 need are 1.5 to 4.2
TWh (Section 3.1) and about 5 TWh (Section 6.2). Both are below this range.
However, the cushion gas share will be site-specific, may range from about 20
to 80%, and will depend on gas composition. At 80%, working gas would be 4.6
TWh (our arithmetic). Cushion gas needs to be excluded from capacity estimates
in more detailed site characterisation.

## Limitations

We assessed technical suitability only. Economic, social, cultural and
regulatory factors can make a site unsuitable. In New Zealand, rights to pore
space, and access to it, also need careful discussion with the indigenous
population. We gave lower weights to parameters whose effect on hydrogen
storage is still uncertain. Our studies suggest that the main risks needing
further research include (1) losses and contamination from hydrogen reacting
with CO2, coal and other materials, and (2) leakage through seals, because
hydrogen diffuses more readily than methane and has a lower viscosity and
density. We aim to select sites for characterisation that can address some of
these risks, and then to revise the parameters, weights and criteria.

## Glossary

**Electrolysis** splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity.

A **terawatt hour (TWh)** is a billion kilowatt hours, a unit of energy.

A **depleted field** is an oil or gas field from which most of the recoverable
petroleum has been produced.

An **aquifer** is a porous rock layer filled with water. Storage is generally
limited to saline aquifers, because of the risk of contaminating drinking
water.

**Pore space** is the small gaps between the grains of a rock, where gas or
water is held.

**Cushion gas** is retained in a storage reservoir to maintain its pressure.
**Working gas** is the part that is injected and withdrawn.

A **caprock**, or top seal, is a low-permeability rock layer above a reservoir
that prevents gas from migrating upward out of it. A **fault seal** is a fault
that prevents gas from flowing across it.

**Permeability** measures how easily fluid flows through rock, here in
**millidarcies (mD)**.

A **gas cap** is a layer of natural gas that lies above oil in the same
reservoir.

**Hydrocarbon phase** records whether a reservoir held gas, gas above oil, or
oil only.

A **lithified** sediment has been compacted and cemented into rock.

The **Paleogene** is the geological period of the Paleocene, Eocene and
Oligocene epochs. The **Miocene** is the younger epoch that followed it.

## Abstract

Seasonal underground hydrogen storage (UHS) in porous media provides an as yet untested method for storing surplus renewable energy and balancing our energy demands. This study investigates the technical suitability for UHS in depleted hydrocarbon fields and one deep aquifer site in Taranaki Basin, Aotearoa New Zealand. Prospective sites are assessed using a decision tree approach, providing a “fast-track” method for identifying potential sites, and a decision matrix approach for ranking optimal sites. Based on expert elicitation, the most important factors to consider are storage capacity, reservoir depth, and parameters that affect hydrogen injectivity/withdrawal and containment. Results from both approaches suggest that Paleogene reservoirs from gas (or gas cap) fields provide the best option for demonstrating UHS in Aotearoa New Zealand, and that the country’s projected 2050 hydrogen storage demand could be exceeded by developing one or two high ranking sites. Lower priority is assigned to heterolithic and typically finer grained, labile and, clay-rich Miocene oil reservoirs, and to deep aquifers that have no proven hydrocarbon containment.
