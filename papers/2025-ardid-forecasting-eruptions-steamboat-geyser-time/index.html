Forecasting Eruptions at Steamboat Geyser: Time Scales, Differentiability, and Detectability of Seismic Precursors Through Data‐Driven Methods

Alberto Ardid, Anna Barth, David Dempsey, Michael Manga, Shane J. Cronin

In brief

A machine learning model trained on signals in the 2018 seismic record of Steamboat Geyser showed a fair ability to separate pre-eruption periods from other times in later data. Its eruption probability peaked at 12.6% within 18 hours of an eruption, compared with 8.1% from eruption intervals alone.

  • Precursors at Steamboat Geyser were most distinct over an 18-hour window, and windows of 6 to 24 hours also separated pre-eruption periods from other times.
  • On 13 eruptions withheld from training, the model scored an AUC of 0.88, where 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect.
  • Models using seismic amplitude alone did not perform as well as the model built on statistical features of the signal.
  • The calibrated probability of an eruption peaked at 12.6% within 18 hours, a marginal increase over a fixed 8.1% from eruption intervals.
  • Performance improved as eruptions were added to training and stabilised at around 9 training eruptions.

The problem

At 10 a.m. on 23 July 2024, a hydrothermal explosion in Biscuit Basin, Yellowstone National Park, threw boiling water, mud and rock into the air. It damaged boardwalks, and dozens of tourists ran for safety (USGS, 2024). Forecasts with a certain degree of accuracy can let park authorities restrict access to vulnerable areas when an eruption is imminent (Erfurt-Cooper, 2018).

Steamboat Geyser, also in Yellowstone, is the tallest active geyser in the world, with eruptions that can exceed 100 m (Bryan, 2018). Its activity is irregular, with intense eruptive phases separated by long dormant intervals (Bryan, 2018; White, 1967). Forecasts based on the time between eruptions, or on thresholds in the strength of ground shaking, may be less suited to sudden changes below ground (Hurwitz, King, et al., 2021; Reed et al., 2024). Our goal in this study was to find precursors in the seismic record of Steamboat and to test whether they can forecast its eruptions.

What we did

We used one seismometer, 340 m from the geyser, between March and December 2018, a period with 31 eruptions. We reduced the ground motion to four measures of shaking strength, each averaged over 1 minute: three frequency bands between 2 and 16 Hz (hertz, or cycles per second) and a ratio of two of them. From each measure we calculated 700 statistical features over 24-hour windows.

Template matching then tested how long before an eruption the precursors appear. A template is the stretch of a feature that ends at one eruption. For each of about 600,000 templates, from 1.5 to 48 hours long, we tested whether its match with the data before other eruptions differed from its match at other times.

Next, we trained a random forest to recognise the 18 hours before an eruption, using data up to mid-September (18 eruptions). The 13 later eruptions were withheld for testing. Because non-eruptive data outnumber pre-eruption data by up to 400 to 1, each of 100 decision trees was trained on a different random sample of the non-eruptive data. The fraction of trees that forecast an eruption is the consensus, an uncalibrated score between 0 and 1. Isotonic regression converted it into a calibrated probability of an eruption in the next 18 hours. We scored the forecasts by their AUC, where 0.5 is no better than chance and 1 is perfect.

What we found

We found that the 18-hour template window best separated pre-eruption periods from other times, although windows from 6 to 24 hours did so only slightly less well. The medium frequency band (4.5 to 8 Hz) appeared most often among the 5% of templates that best separated pre-eruption data.

Over the test period, the consensus was usually close to 0.4, with spikes to about 0.8 that often occurred within one to two days of an eruption. The model scored an AUC of 0.88. This indicates a fair ability to separate pre-eruption periods from other times. None of the models built on seismic amplitude alone performed as well as the random forest.

With 1 to 20 training eruptions, the AUC improved as eruptions were added and stabilised at around 9 training eruptions. A model trained on one eruption gave some predictive power, although with high uncertainty.

Among the features that rose before eruptions was autocorrelation, at lags of 3 and 7 minutes. Whether these lags reflect a physical process needs further analysis. Another was the longest stretch of shaking above its average, which lasted 2 hours before one eruption and 45 minutes before another. Its peaks could reflect extended pressure build-up or accumulation of gas bubbles (Kedar et al., 1996; Rudolph et al., 2012).

The calibrated probability peaked at 12.6% within 18 hours of an eruption, from a background of 7%. This is a marginal increase over the 8.1% given by a fixed model that divides 18 hours by the average interval between eruptions. Our model uses no information about the time since the last eruption. Smoothed over time, the probability rose before the eruptions of 15 October, 31 October and 15 November. Both the smoothed and the calibrated probability remained low before the eruption of 21 November.

A second interval model, based on a Fréchet distribution, raises the probability up to 18% as time passes since the last eruption. It worked well in 2018, when eruption intervals were quite regular. It gave about 10% at the 31 October eruption and about 4% at the mid-October eruption. Its predictive power would likely diminish if intervals became more irregular, as in periods after 2021 (Reed et al., 2024).

As odds, a peak of 12.6% is roughly 1 in 8, against roughly 1 in 12 for the fixed 8.1%.

Limitations

The timescales of precursors appeared to vary little between eruptions. This suggests that they are largely unaffected by external factors. However, our analysis uses one year of data from one seismometer and no environmental data such as temperature, atmospheric pressure or precipitation. It remains possible that these factors influence the timing of eruptions and their precursors. A seismometer closer to Steamboat could improve the reliability of detection.

Direct machine learning forecasts of hydrothermal explosions such as the one at Biscuit Basin are not currently feasible. This is because few of them have been recorded seismically. However, the approach we used to find the type and timescale of precursors may help to develop monitoring tools for geothermal systems.

Glossary

A geyser erupts water and steam episodically.

A precursor is a signal that recurs before eruptions and is rare at other times.

Hz (hertz) is the number of cycles per second in a vibration.

A feature is a statistic calculated from a window of data, such as its mean or its autocorrelation.

Template matching compares the stretch of a feature before one eruption with the rest of the record.

A random forest combines many decision trees, each of which classifies a case by a sequence of threshold tests, and averages their outputs.

The consensus is the fraction of trees that forecast an eruption.

Isotonic regression converts the consensus into a calibrated probability. The conversion is monotonic: a higher consensus never gives a lower probability.

AUC is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, which plots correct warnings against false alarms as the alert threshold changes.

A Fréchet distribution describes eruption intervals whose probability rises to a peak at a typical interval and then falls.

Autocorrelation measures how closely a signal resembles a time-shifted copy of itself.

Abstract

Geyser eruptions provide a test bed for using geophysical data to forecast eruptions and to understand heat and mass transport in hydrothermal systems. We used time series analyses of seismic data at Steamboat Geyser, Yellowstone National Park, to identify short‐term precursors that are recurrent, detectable in real time, distinctly identifiable, as well as being rare during non‐eruptive periods. We analyzed seismic data from March to December 2018 to identify patterns that occurred before 31 eruptions. Four seismic amplitude measures and 700 time‐series features were computed from the seismic data. A template matching analysis identified an optimal 18‐hr window for detecting precursors. We applied a random forest to classify pre‐eruptive and non‐eruptive data for out‐of‐sample eruptions (eruptions that were not included in the model's training data), showing ability to distinguish between the two. This model performed better than a simpler amplitude‐based approach. Seismic features with the most predictive power include autocorrelations, longest strike above the mean, and change quantiles, particularly within the 4.5–16 Hz frequency range. We applied isotonic regression, a method that converts raw model outputs into calibrated probabilities, to improve the interpretability of eruption forecasting outputs. The likelihood of an eruption reaches 12.6% within 18 hr prior to the event, representing a marginal increase over the static 8% probability derived solely from eruption intervals. Unlike the interval‐based approach, our model does not rely on the time since the last eruption, instead using real‐time seismic features to detect precursory signals. Our study advances Machine Learning methodologies in eruption forecasting by integrating calibrated probability estimation through isotonic regression, which has advantages over traditional approaches for geysers with highly irregular eruption intervals.
