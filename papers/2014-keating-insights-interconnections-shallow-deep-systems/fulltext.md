If carbon dioxide (CO2) sequestration into deep geologic reservoirs is to be accepted by the public and environmental regulators, the possibility of upward leakage into shallow groundwater should be acknowledged

and those processes well-understood. Studies of natural CO2 reservoirs and their connec- tion

(or lack thereof) with the shallow subsurface is one way to explore these issues. A natural reservoir near

Springerville, Arizona has leaked CO2 to the surface along a fault zone for thousands of years, creating large

travertine deposits. In recent times, the CO2 leak rates have declined signiﬁcantly yet the shallow aquifer

is still highly enriched in CO2. In this study, using water level data and simulations we demon- strate

that the fault zone likely provides hydrologic communication between the shallow aquifer and the

deeper reservoir. It is reasonable to assume, therefore, that the source of the CO2 in wells completed within the fault zone is the deeper CO2 reservoir. We present water chemistry data to demonstrate the geochemical

impact of this CO2 on shallow groundwater quality. Interestingly, arsenic concentrations are

elevated, but other trace metals concentrations are not. Arsenic and chloride concentrations co-vary, suggesting

perhaps an external source of both elements rather than an in situ release of As due to CO2 attack on shallow aquifer sediments. Observations at this site demonstrate that hydraulic communica- tions

between shallow and deep layers and upward CO2 migration does not preclude long-term viability of

a substantial CO2 reservoir at depth. We present multi-phase ﬂow simulations to illustrate possible mechanisms

trapping the CO2 at depth. Collectively, these analyses show that some degree of upward CO2 leakage may not be necessarily incompatible with the overarching goals of sequestering CO2 and protecting

shallow groundwater. Published

1. Introduction

Injection of anthropogenic carbon dioxide (CO2) into the sub- surface has been suggested as an important component of a multi-faceted effort to reduce greenhouse gases (DOE). Carbon dioxide has been successfully injected into depleted oil ﬁelds for decades, but demonstrations of successful CO2 injection into other deep reservoirs, such as saline aquifers, are fairly few and all rela- tively

recent (Korbol and Kaddour, 1995; Preisig and Prevost, 2011; Hovorka et al., 2006, 2011). Of course it is not sufﬁcient merely to inject the CO2; a successful sequestration project will have to demonstrate that almost all of the CO2 stays in the deep reservoir and, if any leaks upward, that it does not negatively impact shal- low drinking water resources. natural CO2 reservoir near Springerville, Arizona to shed light on both long-term storage and upward leakage. Natural-occurring gas reservoirs are distributed throughout the world and have been exploited primarily for methane, noble gases, and carbon dioxide. The fact that these reservoirs have existed for millions of years has been used to argue that injected CO2 can be sta- ble in deep reservoirs and that caprocks can maintain their integrity for long periods of time. Some natural reservoirs, however, do leak gases to the surface. Understanding the mechanisms of leakage and the impact of the leakage on shallow groundwater resources using a ‘leaky’ natural reservoir near Springerville, Arizona is the focus of this study. Additionally, we address the question ‘how is it possible

that a substantial volume of gas-phase CO2 can be stored underground in the presence of a large leakage pathway?’ Previous studies of the impact of CO2 sequestration on shallow groundwater resources have focused on two issues: (1) CO2 intru- sion into the shallow aquifer, which may lower groundwater pH and mobilize trace metals (Little and Jackson, 2010; Kharaka et al.,

2010), and/or (2) brine intrusion, which would introduce salts and trace metals into the groundwater (Keating et al., 2010). The leak- age pathway could be an abandoned well, a fault, or a localized caprock breach. Leakage pathways could be natural or ‘activated’ by overpressurization due to injection. Simulation and natural analog studies have addressed the physical mechanism of brine intrusion (Birkholzer et al., 2009; Oldenburg et al., 2011; Keating et al., 2012). Oldenburg et al. (2011) concluded that if overpressurization is mod- erate and salinity is high, upward brine migration will be limited and a new steady state can be achieved with the fresh water/saline water boundary well below the bottom of the aquifer. However, if overpressurization is high or salinity is low, brine will migrate into the shallow aquifer. Keating et al. (2012) used simulations and contrasting observations at two natural analog sites to conclude that the geometry of the leaky pathway will affect the likelihood of brine intrusion. They concluded that narrow features (wellbores, narrow fault zones) are more likely to convey brine into the shallow aquifer than broad leaky features. Birkholzer et al. (2009) analyzed an idealized multi-layer aquifer/aquitard system without a vertical conduit and concluded that brine intrusion would be unlikely in such a setting. The

impact of brine and CO2 intrusion on water quality in the shallow aquifer has been the focus of many recent studies. Lab- oratory experiments where sediments are exposed to CO2 have established the potential for trace metal release (Little and Jackson, 2010; Smyth et al., 2009). Likewise, controlled ﬁeld experiments have established that CO2 release can cause increases in trace metal concentrations (Kharaka et al., 2010; Trautz et al., 2013, Peter et al., 2012). In most cases, the increase in trace metal concentrations was very subtle and concentrations did not approach or exceed drink- ing water standards. Some laboratory experiments (Carey et al., 2009) and natural analog studies (Keating et al., 2013) suggest that trace metal elevation will be temporary. As buffering reactions proceed, trace metals can precipitate or re-adsorb (Aiuppa et al., 2005). Apart from trace metals, saline brines can be a signiﬁcant threat to ground-water and NaCl will not be subject to signiﬁcant buffering. It is likely that each site will have a unique geochemical response to CO2 leakage.

2. Site description

The Springerville–St. Johns dome is a shallow (200–700 m) nat- ural CO2 reservoir near the southern edge of the Colorado Plateau (Moore et al., 2005), reported to contain as much as 445 billion m3

of CO2 (Stevens et al., 2005). The CO2 is closely associated with a relatively young volcanic ﬁeld (<3 Ma) (Moore et al., 2005). The reservoir is hosted in an alternating sequence of discontinuous sandstone, limestone, shale, and anhydrite layers of the Permian Supai Group. Below the Supai is fractured Precambrian granite basement; above are the Glorieta Sandstone and San Andres Lime- stone, which are regionally extensive aquifers. These, in turn, are capped by conﬁning layers (shales of the Moenkopi and Chinle For- mations). Northwest trending faults and related folds also cross the dome, with the Coyote Wash fault bounding the western margin of the dome. The Tuscon Electric Power (TEP) company wells produce groundwater from the Glorieta Sandstone (equivalent to the Coconino Sandstone). Regionally, this sandstone unit is the most productive unit of the C aquifer, the predominant groundwa- ter source in the Colorado Plateau/Little Colorado River basin of Arizona and western New Mexico (Hoffmann et al., 2006). The production wells are located in the Coyote Wash fault zone (see Fig. 1). The wells have high levels of dissolved CO2, which has been problematic

Previous work- ers have concluded that the elevated dissolved CO2 observed in the TEP groundwater wells and at Salado Springs is sourced from the underlying CO2 dome, and that this CO2 is migrating upwards primarily along the Coyote Wash Fault zone (Gilﬁllan et al., 2011; Moore et al., 2005). Furthermore, the extensive travertine deposits in the area are attributed to CO2–rock–water interaction mov- ing carbonate-saturated groundwater to the surface along faults (Crumpler et al., 1994). The noble gas and stable isotope geochem- istry of the CO2 source and dissolved gases in the groundwater suggests a mantle-derived magmatic source for the CO2 that is likely associated with the Springerville volcanic ﬁeld (Gilﬁllan et al., 2008, 2011). Allis et al. (2005) conducted multi-phase reactive-transport simulations of the reservoir, leakage along the fault zone, and travertine deposition. They were able to explain the timing and total volume of travertine deposition by assuming an inﬂux of CO2 into the base of the fault (from below) of ∼63 kTon/year CO2. Their simulations showed a transient overpressurization of the reservoir, leading to a rise in the water table elevation and broad area of water and CO2 discharge. Less is known about the migration of brine from the reservoir layers into the shallow aquifer. The highest measured salinity in the aquifer (2150 ppm, Moore et al., 2005) is elevated compared to regional groundwater but much less saline than deeper water (at least 4210 ppm and probably much higher due to anhydrite lay- ers). Compared to the C aquifer chemistry at locations distal to the Springerville–St. Johns CO2 system, the salinity observed here is sig- niﬁcantly higher. For example, 200 km northwest of Springerville, the C aquifer has a chloride levels <115 mg/l, with arsenic lev- els below the maximum contaminant level as deﬁned by the U.S. EPA (Hoffmann et al., 2006). One, although the integrity of the caprock is sufﬁcient to preserve a signiﬁcant reservoir of CO2 (and high helium, ∼1%, Rauzi, 2003) within the deeper sedimentary units, high levels of deep-source dissolved CO2 in the shallow aquifer demonstrate a pathway for CO2 migration. A likely pathway to explain the CO2 occurrence in shallow water is the nearby relatively broad, high-permeability fault zone (Coyote Wash Fault zone). These characteristics have combined to make the Springerville–St. Johns CO2 dome a natural analog for leakage from a geologic carbon sequestration storage site. A variety of questions can be asked including (1) can the CO2 be identiﬁed in the shallow groundwater; (2) can CO2 migration along the fault zone explain the levels of dissolved CO2 seen in the aquifer; (3) is the CO2 migrating alone or with brine; and (4) has the leakage from the CO2 storage site affected the shallow groundwater chemistry?

3. Water chemistry

3.1. Methods

Groundwater samples were collected from 8 wells that Tucson Electric Power uses to produce cooling water for the Springerville Generating Station (Fig. 1). These 8 wells were in service at the time of sampling. Two travertine spring mounds and one shallow alluvial well were also sampled at Salado Springs (Fig. 1). The production wells operate at high ﬂow rates so purging of the well bore was unnecessary, and these wells are closed systems at the well-head location preventing degassing and oxygenation during pumping to the surface. These wells are also constructed of stainless steel to minimize corrosion from CO2-charged groundwater. Water was collected from taps located either on the wellhead or on piping leading directly from the wellhead. Prior to sample collection, water

was allowed to ﬂow slowly (to minimize degassing) until tempera- ture, pH and conductivity stabilized, usually within a few minutes. At Salado Springs, grab samples were collected from the two spring mounds. The alluvial well was under slight artesian pressure so it produced water from a spigot that was allowed to discharge until ﬁeld parameters stabilized. Samples for major cation and trace metals analyses were ﬁeld ﬁltered (0.45 m) and acidiﬁed with trace metal grade nitric acid. Samples for anions, alkalinity and sta- ble isotope analyses were collected unﬁltered with no headspace. Carbon stable isotope samples were collected into gas tight bottles to prevent degassing and fractionation prior to analysis. All samples were processed and analyzed at the Los Alamos National Laboratory Earth and Environmental Sciences Geochem- istry and Geomaterials Research Laboratories (GGRL). Major cations and trace metals were measured by ICP-OES and ICP-MS, and anions were determined by Ion Chromatography. Stable isotope ratios of dissolved inorganic carbon, and oxygen and hydrogen in water were determined by continuous ﬂow isotope ratio mass spectrom- etry (CF-IRMS) on a GV Instruments Isoprime mass spectrometer attached to a Multiﬂow peripheral instrument. Results are reported using standard delta notation in per mil (‰). ı13C is reported versus PDB, and ı18O and ıD are reported versus SMOW. Standard devi- ations on ı13C, ı18O and ıD measurements based on in-house and international standards were 0.13, 0.12 and 0.5‰ respectively.

The primary importance of dissolved CO2 transport rather than free-phase transport is sig- niﬁcant.

Also of interest is the source of salinity and trace elements creat- ing the observed mixing trends. The values of chloride and arsenic (491 and 0.042 mg/l) are higher than background values (<2 g/l) observed in waters to the north (Hoffmann et al., 2006). Emplace- ment of the Springerville volcanic ﬁeld could have introduced trace elements such as As, Li and B due to magmatic devolatilization and local hydrothermal circulation (Condit et al., 1989; Guilbert and Park, 1986). However, this cannot explain the addition of signif- icant amounts of chloride, sulfate and other major elements (e.g., calcium, sodium). A more plausible source of brine and trace metals is the Supai Group encountered along ﬂow paths below the aquifer. High arsenic values up to 1000 ppb in Verde Valley springs south- west of Springerville, are attributed to sources within the Supai Group (Foust et al., 2004; Johnson et al., 2012). Evaporite horizons, including thick anhydrite beds in the Big A Butte Member show evi- dence of CO2-brine-rock interaction (e.g., formation of secondary dawsonite) (Moore et al., 2005) are potential sources of these trace elements in the Supai Group. Modern salars are known to concen- trate trace elements such as As, B, and Li and result in associated groundwater enrichment in these elements (Concha et al., 2010; Risacher and Fritz, 1991). A notable exception to the binary mixing trends is the concen- tration of arsenic at Salado Springs (Fig. 4). Based on other major and trace elements, Salado Springs appears quite similar to wells P-9a and P-18 that deﬁne the high concentration end member. In summary, evidence from the water chemistry measurements is consistent with a conceptual model of brackish or saline water and CO2 moving up along the fault zone. The water transports salinity and trace metals from the layers within the Supai Group. This suggests the CO2 moved upward in the dissolved phase, and degassed as pressures decreases or as the water mixed with shallow groundwater. The common theme of upward ﬂow of CO2-enriched water explains the transport of CO2, major and trace elements in this system. This does not preclude the possibility that CO2-induced changes in shallow water quality might be occurring here, rather, such changes are probably much more subtle than the changes caused by mixing with saline water.

6. Fault permeability

We used hydrologic data and groundwater ﬂow simulations to explore the hydrologic characteristics of the fault system. This system is unique in that production wells are completed near the fault, and complete extraction and drawdown data are avail- able for the wellﬁeld. A substantial amount of water has been produced from these wells (see Fig. 6) with relatively minor draw- down (34 m at most; see Fig. 1). An interesting feature of the drawdown response is that monitoring wells on the upthrown side of the fault (to the north) have experienced no measurable drawdown (see Fig. 1). We developed a series of 3-D ground- water ﬂow simulations with the goal of developing and testing hypotheses about the hydrologic characteristics of the fault zones, and attempting to match the drawdown data (to ﬁrst order). There is considerable uncertainty about the properties of the rock layers and the fault zone; to our knowledge there are no site- speciﬁc hydraulic testing results available. Therefore, for each conceptual model of the fault system, we consider the hydrologic properties of the rocks to be uncertain parameters that can be optimized to best match measured drawdowns. If two concep- tual models match the drawdowns equally well, they are equally likely.

7. The unlikely co-existence of a gas reservoir and a broad, leaky conduit

There are multiple

lines of evidence, including new analyses presented here, that the St. Johns–Springerville CO2 reservoir is penetrated by a broad, highly permeable fault zone. How, then, can an estimated 445 billion m3 of buoyant CO2 gas remain at depth? This question is

directly relevant to engineered CO2 sequestra- tion. Here we present multi-phase (CO2/water) ﬂow simulations to

investigate the stability of a shallow gas reservoir in the presence of a wide leaky fault. To simplify the system we extracted a 2-D cross-section from the 3-D model described above. The cross-section is 100 m thick, and represents approximately 1/500th the total reservoir vol- ume (see Fig. 9). We initialized the simulations with a gas-phase CO2 reservoir in multiple layers, according to the conceptual and numerical models presented by Allis et al. (2005). The pore space in the gas reservoir is 60% free-phase CO2, 40% water. The gas is allowed to dissolve in the water up to the pressure/temperature- dependent solubility limit. The total CO2 mass of the initial reservoir is ∼7 × 108 kg, approximately 30% in the dissolved phase. Pressure is initially hydrostatic, with a slight horizontal gradient in the shal- low

aquifer (.028). This gradient is within the range of measured gradients in the shallow aquifer at Springerville (approximately 0–0.03; Fig. 1). Figs. 10 and 11 illustrates the rapid changes that occur in the ﬁrst 300 days of simulation. Due to buoyancy, the gas begins to move upward along the broad high-permeability fault zone. Quickly, the fault zone is ﬁlled with a dissolved CO2 plume, which eventually moves away from the fault in the shallow aquifer. After a short time, however, the rate of gas depletion of the reservoir sharply declines.

An interesting aspect of these results is that a large plume of dissolved CO2 is maintained within the fault zone long after the primary upward pulse of buoyancy-driven gas phase. The geom- etry of this plume is consistent with the presence of high levels of dissolved CO2 in the TEP wells. The slow leak rate of CO2 gas (Fig. 12) is probably adequate to maintain this dissolved CO2 plume indeﬁnitely.

Although we used some of the features of the Springerville site, such as the hydrostratigraphy and fault geometry, to inform the simulations, these simulations are not meant to replicate trans- port of CO2 speciﬁcally at the site, historically or modern. Rather, they are designed to address generic questions of reservoir sta- bility in the presence of faults. For example one key difference between these simulations and the evolution of the Springerville site is that the fault did not abruptly open after the CO2 was emplaced. Another key difference is that CO2 discharged to the surface along the fault zone at Springerville, whereas in these sim- ulations CO2 can only move laterally in the shallow groundwater aquifer.

Although this simulation does not mimic actual processes of reservoir development at Springerville, it does provide insights into the mechanisms allowing buoyant CO2 gas to stay in place despite the presence of vertical leaky conduits. Once the gas within and near the leaky feature escapes to the shallow aquifer, the remaining gas reservoir leaks at a very slow rate. Future studies would be use- ful for determining the impact of speciﬁc factors such as caprock thickness, reservoir thickness, and radial distance to the fault for gas reservoir stability.

8. Conclusions

The CO2 reservoir at Springerville–St Johns provides a natural analog for understanding mechanisms of long-term CO2 storage and leakage. Additionally, new insights can be gained regarding

E. Keating et al. / International Journal of Greenhouse Gas Control 25 (2014) 162–172 171

the impacts of CO2 leakage on shallow groundwater systems. The primary mechanism of long-term CO2 storage at this site, and many other natural gas reservoirs has been described previously as ‘sol- ubility trapping’ (Gilﬁllan et al., 2009). At this site, there appears to be a substantial amount of CO2 stored in both the dissolved and free (gas) phase (Rauzi, 1999). The simulations here show that buoyant free phase CO2 can remain at depth despite the presence of a large scale, high permeability leakage path. This is true even when caprock layers are nearly horizontal. This is because, under hydrostatic conditions, there is insufﬁcient driving force for the free phase CO2 to move laterally toward the fault before it can escape vertically.

Analysis of major, minor element geochemistry and stable iso- topes of carbon demonstrates that the leakage pathway (Coyote Wash Fault zone) has been a conduit for upward movement of CO2 dissolved in brackish or saline water. CO2 has degassed as the water moved upwards. Possible mechanisms causing upward movement of CO2-saturated water include: (1) overpressuriza- tion at depth due to mantle-derived CO2 inﬂow from basement penetrating faults (e.g., Crossey et al., 2009) or (2) groundwater overpressurization due to past glacial to interglacial transitions (e.g.,

Kampman et al., 2012). Importantly, this differs from the sce- nario presented by Keating et al. (2010), which considered free phase CO2 rising along the fault and displacing brine. Here, the primary transport mechanism is upward ﬂow of dissolved phase CO2.

The measured history of water extraction and consequence pressure declines at a wellﬁeld in the fault zone provided the oppor- tunity to characterize the hydrologic characteristics of the fault zone. Inverse modeling demonstrates that the offset of strata due to faulting is itself sufﬁcient explanation for the hydrologic compart- mentalization of TEP monitoring wells to the northeast. However, the fault zone must be broad, permeable, and deep to explain the measured drawdowns given the levels of production over the past few decades. This conclusion does not preclude the possibility that other conceptual models, not tested here, could be equally valid for the site. Elevated and co-varying concentrations of As, Li, B, Rb, Na, SO4 and Cl in the shallow aquifer demonstrate some degree of upward transport of brine or brackish water. The fairly linear relationship between trace elements and Cl suggest trace metals were not mobi- lized in situ due to CO2 intrusion. This result does not preclude the possibility that this might be the case at other sites. Carbon isotopes suggest that this water was likely saturated with CO2 and degassed as it moved upward along the fault. As noted by previous authors, the mass rate of CO2 migrating to the shallow aquifer is presum- ably much lower than 10’s to 100’s of thousands of years ago when extensive travertine deposits were created. Salado Springs are cur- rently undersaturated with respect to carbonates (SI, Table 1) and are precipitating travertine today only in small amounts as spring waters degas. Nevertheless, upward transport is still occurring. There are several possible implications of this study for engi- neered carbon sequestration and (possible) leakage. First, a breach in the caprock or activated fault may release some CO2 to the sur- face but buoyancy alone will not be sufﬁcient to release CO2 distant from the leakage pathway. Second, the release of CO2 to the shal- low aquifer will not necessarily mobilize trace metals in the shallow aquifer. Our measurements at this site do not support such a mecha- nism. Third, as previous authors have suggested, mobilization and trapping of CO2 in the dissolved phase will be the most impor- tant process (Gilﬁllan et al., 2009). At Springerville–St Johns dome, present or past pressure conditions forced brackish or saline water upward along the fault, and with it dissolved CO2. A signiﬁcant amount of degassing occurred as the CO2 rose. These processes are

quite different than the conceptual model of buoyancy-driven gas-phase ﬂow in a leakage scenario.

This site could be useful for further ﬁeld and simulation stud- ies aimed at better understanding CO2 ﬂow and transport in the subsurface. For example, alternative conceptual models of the site could be tested more rigorously using integrated multi-phase ﬂow/reactive transport simulations and comparison of model results to stable isotope and trace element data. New insights might be gained by sampling a larger number of wells in the area, and ana- lyzing for other trace gases such as helium. Finally, issues related to heterogeneity of fault zones, and impact on CO2 transport could be explored with focused studies of this site.
