## In brief

At a natural CO2 reservoir in Arizona, a permeable fault likely connects the deep gas to a shallow aquifer. Water chemistry suggests that CO2 rises dissolved in saline water that also contains arsenic. In our simulations, most of the gas remains at depth despite the fault.

- A groundwater model matched drawdowns from decades of pumping best when the fault zone was distinct, permeable and extended through the full model thickness.
- Chloride, sulfate, bicarbonate, lithium, rubidium and arsenic rise together in the production wells, consistent with saline, CO2-rich water from depth mixing with dilute groundwater.
- Arsenic was the only trace element above the US drinking water limit, reaching 0.042 mg/l, about four times the 0.010 mg/l limit.
- In a simple degassing model, a loss of 40–50% of the dissolved CO2 can account for the carbon isotopes of the most concentrated groundwater.
- Simulated gas rose along the fault within 300 days. Scaled to a 50 km fault, the later leak would empty a dome-sized reservoir in about 2,800 years without resupply.

## The problem

A project that stores carbon dioxide (CO2) in deep rock has to show that almost
all of the CO2 is retained at depth, and that any CO2 that leaks upward does
not harm shallow drinking water. Natural CO2 reservoirs can inform both
questions.

The Springerville–St. Johns dome in Arizona is a natural CO2 reservoir 200 to
700 m below the surface (Moore et al., 2005). It is reported to hold up to 445
billion m³ of CO2, measured at atmospheric pressure (Stevens et al., 2005). The
Coyote Wash fault zone crosses it. CO2 has leaked to the surface along this
fault for thousands of years and formed travertine deposits. The leak rate has
since declined, but the shallow aquifer is still rich in dissolved CO2. High
dissolved CO2 in the groundwater wells in the fault zone has required their
operator to replace the pipe in most of them with stainless steel because of
corrosion.

Our goal in this study was to ask (1) whether the fault connects the deep
reservoir to the shallow aquifer, (2) what the leaking CO2 has done to
groundwater quality, and (3) how up to 445 billion m³ of CO2 can remain stored
underground beside a broad, permeable fault.

## What we did

We sampled water from 8 wells that Tucson Electric Power pumps for cooling
water at the Springerville Generating Station, and from two travertine spring
mounds and a shallow well at Salado Springs.

Pumping from the wellfield between 1981 and 2010 lowered water levels by up to
34 m. Monitoring wells on the north side of the fault showed no measurable
drawdown. We built a three-dimensional groundwater flow model with the FEHM
code (Zyvoloski, 1997) and tested three versions of the fault zone. The first
treated the fault zone as no different from the surrounding rock layers ("flow
neutral"). The second was a distinct zone reaching only to the base of the
deepest production well, and the third extended through the full thickness of
the model. For each version, the code PEST adjusted rock permeabilities to best
match the drawdown at 21 wells.

To investigate the stability of a shallow gas reservoir beside a wide,
permeable fault, we ran multi-phase (CO2 and water) simulations on a
two-dimensional cross-section, 100 m thick, taken from the flow model.

## What we found

The distinct fault zone through the full model thickness matched the drawdowns
best. Its sum of squared errors between modelled and measured drawdown was
598 at the 15 production wells. This is about half the 1,087 of the
flow-neutral fault and a ninth of the 5,358 of the shallow fault zone (our
arithmetic). At the 6 monitoring wells its sum was 6, against 133 and 10. This analysis demonstrates that the fault that carried CO2 to the
surface in the past is still a pathway for groundwater from depth.

In the shallow aquifer, chloride, bicarbonate, sulfate, lithium and rubidium
rise and fall together. This pattern is consistent with a mixture of two
waters, a saltier, CO2-rich water rising along the fault and more dilute
groundwater. Arsenic was the only trace element above the US Environmental
Protection Agency drinking water limit of 0.010 mg/l. It reached 0.042 mg/l in
one well and exceeded the limit in 9 of the 11 samples (our count from Table
2). Because arsenic rises with chloride in the production wells, we suggest
that it arrives with the saline water. The fairly linear relationship with
chloride also suggests that CO2 has not released trace metals from the aquifer
sediments themselves. However, subtler changes in water quality driven by CO2
might still be occurring here. At Salado Springs arsenic falls below the
trend. Microbial processes that precipitate iron oxides could lower it there.

In a simple degassing model, a loss of 40–50% of the dissolved CO2 can account
for the carbon isotope ratio and dissolved carbon content of the most
concentrated groundwater. We suggest that CO2 moved upward dissolved in
brackish or saline water and degassed as the pressure fell or as the water
mixed with shallow groundwater.

In the simulations, buoyant gas rose along the fault within the first 300 days.
Once the gas in and near the fault had escaped, the rate at which the reservoir
lost gas fell to a nearly constant 6 × 10⁵ kg (600 tonnes) per year for the 100
m slice. This is because, at hydrostatic pressure, there is insufficient
driving force to move the gas sideways to the fault before it can escape
upward. Scaled to a 50 km length of fault, this rate would empty a reservoir
the size of the dome in about 2,800 years if no new CO2 arrived from the
mantle. That slow leak is probably enough to maintain a plume of dissolved CO2
in the fault zone indefinitely. At these rates, some upward leakage of CO2 may
be compatible with storing CO2 at depth and protecting shallow groundwater.

## Limitations

The multi-phase simulations are not meant to reproduce the history of the site.
In our model the fault was present from the start and did not open after the
CO2 was emplaced. Our fault model is calibrated to drawdown data alone, because
we know of no site-specific hydraulic tests. Conceptual models that we did not
test could be equally valid.

Integrated multi-phase flow and reactive transport simulations, compared
against the isotope and trace element data, could test alternative models of
the site more rigorously.

## Glossary

**Carbon dioxide (CO2) storage**, or sequestration, is the injection of CO2
into deep rock to isolate it from the atmosphere.

An **aquifer** is a layer of rock or sediment that holds and yields usable
groundwater.

A **fault zone** is a band of broken rock along which the ground on either side
has moved.

**Travertine** is a limestone deposited from spring water rich in dissolved
CO2.

**Drawdown** is the fall in the water level in a well caused by pumping.

A **production well** pumps water out of the ground, and a **wellfield** is a
group of them. A **monitoring well** is used only to measure the water level.

A **conceptual model** is a hypothesis about how a system is arranged and
works, here about the hydraulic properties of the fault zone.

**Permeability** is a measure of how easily fluid flows through a rock.

**FEHM** (Finite Element Heat and Mass Transfer) is a computer code that
simulates the flow of water, gas and heat through rock.

**PEST** is a computer code that adjusts model parameters to best match
measurements.

The **sum of squared errors** adds up the squared differences between modelled
and measured values. A smaller sum is a better match.

A **multi-phase** simulation models the flow of more than one fluid, here CO2
gas and water.

**Buoyant** gas is less dense than the water around it and tends to rise.

**Hydrostatic** pressure is the pressure of a still column of water.

A **trace element** is an element present in water at low concentration, such
as arsenic or lithium.

**Milligrams per litre (mg/l)** is a unit of concentration, the mass of a
substance dissolved in one litre of water.

**Brackish** water is saltier than fresh water but less salty than seawater.
**Saline** water has a high concentration of dissolved salt.

**Isotopes** are forms of an element with different masses. The ratio of carbon
isotopes in water indicates the source of its carbon and the processes that
have affected it.

**Degassing** is the loss of dissolved gas from water as a separate gas phase.

**Iron oxides** are minerals of iron and oxygen that can take up arsenic as
they form.

The **mantle** is the layer of the Earth below the crust.

**Reactive transport** simulations model the flow of water together with the
chemical reactions between the water and the rock.

A **plume** is a body of fluid, here water carrying dissolved CO2, moving
through the rock.

## Abstract

If carbon dioxide (CO2) sequestration into deep geologic reservoirs is to be accepted by the public and environmental regulators, the possibility of upward leakage into shallow groundwater should be acknowledged and those processes well-understood. Studies of natural CO2 reservoirs and their connection (or lack thereof) with the shallow subsurface is one way to explore these issues. A natural reservoir near Springerville, Arizona has leaked CO2 to the surface along a fault zone for thousands of years, creating large travertine deposits. In recent times, the CO2 leak rates have declined significantly yet the shallow aquifer is still highly enriched in CO2. In this study, using water level data and simulations we demonstrate that the fault zone likely provides hydrologic communication between the shallow aquifer and the deeper reservoir. It is reasonable to assume, therefore, that the source of the CO2 in wells completed within the fault zone is the deeper CO2 reservoir. We present water chemistry data to demonstrate the geochemical impact of this CO2 on shallow groundwater quality. Interestingly, arsenic concentrations are elevated, but other trace metals concentrations are not. Arsenic and chloride concentrations co-vary, suggesting perhaps an external source of both elements rather than an in situ release of As due to CO2 attack on shallow aquifer sediments. Observations at this site demonstrate that hydraulic communications between shallow and deep layers and upward CO2 migration does not preclude long-term viability of a substantial CO2 reservoir at depth. We present multi-phase flow simulations to illustrate possible mechanisms trapping the CO2 at depth. Collectively, these analyses show that some degree of upward CO2 leakage may not be necessarily incompatible with the overarching goals of sequestering CO2 and protecting shallow groundwater.
