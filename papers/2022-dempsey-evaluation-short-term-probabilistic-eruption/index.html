Evaluation of short-term probabilistic eruption forecasting at Whakaari, New Zealand

In brief

We ran an eruption forecaster at Whakaari for 18 months and filtered out the earthquakes that caused its three false positives. In a test with the 2019 eruption withheld from training, a calibrated version gave a 2 to 4% chance in 48 hours, up to 400 times its lowest level.

  • Between February 2020 and August 2021 our real-time forecaster raised three false positives, all caused by regional earthquakes. No eruptions occurred.
  • With earthquake filtering and data normalisation added, the improved model would have raised none of those three false positives.
  • A warning system tested on eruptions withheld from training warned of six of seven eruptions, with warnings in effect about 14 days a year.
  • During a warning the chance of eruption was 8.1% in 48 hours, 126 times higher than outside a warning.
  • Over 2 to 18 December 2019, expert estimates of a second eruption scored worse than a fixed forecast based on the 2011 to 2019 average eruption rate.

The problem

On 9 December 2019, an eruption at Whakaari (White Island) killed 22 tourists and guides and severely injured 26 others. New Zealand scientists set a volcanic alert level, which describes current activity but does not estimate the likelihood of an eruption. They also estimate eruption probabilities by expert elicitation, usually every 1 to 3 months. One week before the 2019 eruption, this gave an 8 to 14% chance of eruption in the following four weeks (GeoNet, 2019b).

Our goal here was to test in real time a machine learning model from an earlier study (Dempsey et al., 2020), which converts seismic data into an eruption score, and to turn that score into a probability that can be compared with expert judgement.

What we did

We ran the forecaster from February 2020 to August 2021 on data from one seismometer. Every 10 minutes it converted the previous 48 hours of volcanic tremor into a score between 0 and 1. A higher score means a higher likelihood of eruption, although it is not a probability.

No eruptions occurred, although the model raised three false positives, all caused by regional earthquakes: a swarm 85 km north of the island in 2020, and three earthquakes of magnitude 7.3 to 8.1 east of New Zealand and near Raoul Island in March 2021. We added a filter that removes short bursts of earthquake shaking before the tremor is averaged. We also normalised the four tremor measures (three frequency bands and one ratio) so that each has a comparable influence.

A warning system issues a warning when the score reaches a trigger value and cancels it after 48 hours below that value. A calibrated model converts each score to a probability using how often past scores of that size were followed by an eruption. For example, a score of about 0.85 occurred 203 times in the training data, and 11 of those were followed by an eruption within 48 hours, or 5.4%. We tested both models pseudo-prospectively, training on all but one eruptive period and forecasting the one withheld.

An uninformed forecast uses only the average eruption rate. From January 2011 to November 2019, six eruptions occurred in 1617 two-day periods, a probability of 0.37% per 48 hours, or about 1 in 270. Our forecast skill score is scaled so that 1 is a perfect forecast, 0 matches the uninformed forecast, and negative values are worse.

What we found

Rerun with these changes, the model raised none of the three false positives. At a trigger of 0.88, the warning system warned of six of the seven eruptions, with warnings in effect about 14 days a year. During a warning the chance of eruption was 8.1% in 48 hours, or about 1 in 12, and outside a warning it was 0.064%, about 1 in 1600, or 126 times lower. However, the system missed the April 2016 eruption.

The warning for the 2019 eruption began at a strong tremor spike about 16 hours beforehand, short of the 48-hour target. In the preceding four days, the calibrated model gave a 2 to 4% chance of eruption in 48 hours, 200 to 400 times its background of 0.01%, or 1 in 10,000. This background is the model's lowest output, which applies to 23% of the study period, whereas 0.37% is an average over all periods. Over 2011 to 2019, the calibrated model scored 0.21 and the warning system 0.12, both better than the uninformed forecast. Over 2 to 18 December 2019, the calibrated model scored 0.33 and the warning system −0.02, about the same as the uninformed forecast.

Converted to a 48-hour window, the 2 December expert estimate was 0.6 to 1.1%. Given that probabilities in this study range over a factor of about a thousand, this broadly agrees with the calibrated model. After the eruption, experts estimated the daily chance of a second explosive eruption. Between 11 and 14 December they put it at 40 to 60%. Over 2 to 18 December, the elicitation scored −1.0 or −1.5, depending on whether its low or high estimates were used. These scores are not directly comparable, because our models were tested with some knowledge of past eruptions, whereas the experts worked in real time.

Possible reasons for the poor elicitation score are (1) a second eruption was likely and did not occur by chance, (2) a 24-hour window was unfamiliar to experts used to windows of weeks to months, and (3) the estimates may have been conservatively inflated, subconsciously or otherwise, because they were used to assess the risk to response teams.

Limitations

Apart from the 18-month real-time test, our results are retrospective and should be read as hypothetical. Using only the previous 48 hours of data from one station, the forecaster cannot use a weeks-long precursor that preceded the 2016 eruption (Ardid et al., 2022). The calibrated probabilities reached a few percent at most before eruptions. We attribute this partly to noise in the seismic data.

In the one sequence tested, retraining the model immediately after the first eruption of October 2013 improved its forecast of the second. However, adopting this would mean restarting prospective testing (Whitehead et al., 2022). We scored the elicitation only for December 2019, because past elicited probabilities are not routinely made public. Finally, a lower trigger value warns of more eruptions but keeps warnings in effect for longer. We argue that this choice should be made by emergency management authorities (Papale, 2017).

Glossary

The volcanic alert level is New Zealand's scale from 0 (no unrest) to 5 (major eruption), describing current activity at a volcano.

Expert elicitation pools the judgements of several scientists into a range of eruption probabilities for a stated time window.

Volcanic tremor is continuous seismic shaking at an active volcano, here averaged over 10-minute intervals.

A false positive is a warning or high forecast that is not followed by an eruption.

An eruptive period is a single eruption or a group of eruptions close in time, treated as one event.

Normalisation rescales several data streams to a common range so that none dominates because of its size.

Pseudo-prospective testing trains a model without one eruption and then uses it to forecast that eruption. This imitates a forecast made before the event.

The uninformed forecast assigns every 48-hour period the same probability, the average eruption rate.

The forecast skill score measures how well probabilities matched outcomes, scaled here so that 0 equals the uninformed forecast.

Abstract

Phreatic explosions at volcanoes are difficult to forecast but can be locally devastating, as illustrated by the deadly 2019 Whakaari (New Zealand) eruption. Quantifying eruption likelihood is essential for risk calculations that underpin volcano access decisions and disaster response. But estimating eruption probabilities is notoriously difficult for sudden onset eruptions. Here, we describe two retrospectively developed models for short-term (48 h) probabilistic forecasting of phreatic eruptions at Whakaari. The models are based on a pseudo-prospective analysis of seven Whakaari eruptions whose precursors were identified by time series feature engineering of continuous seismic data. The first model, an optimized warning system, could anticipate six out of seven eruptions at the cost of 14 warning days each year. While a warning is in effect, the probability of eruption is about 8% in 48 h, which is about 126 times higher than outside the warning. The second model used isotonic calibration to translate the output of the forecast model onto a probability scale. When applied pseudo-prospectively in the 48 h prior to the December 2019 eruption, it indicated an eruption probability up to 400 times higher than the background. Finally, we quantified the accuracy of these seismic data-driven forecasts, alongside an observatory expert elicitation that used multiple data sources. To do this, we used a forecast skill score that was benchmarked against the average rate of eruptions at Whakaari between 2011 and 2019. This exercise highlights the conditions under which the three different forecasting approaches perform well and where potential improvements could be made.
