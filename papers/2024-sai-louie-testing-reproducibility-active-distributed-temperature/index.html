Testing the reproducibility of active-distributed temperature sensing for measuring groundwater specific discharge beneath a braided river

Alice J. Sai Louie, Leanne K. Morgan, Eddie W. Banks, David Dempsey, Scott Wilson

The problem

River loss, the water that rivers lose to the aquifers beneath them, is a major source of groundwater recharge at the global scale (Döll and Fiedler, 2008). The braided Wairau River in New Zealand's South Island, for example, supplies about 94% of the recharge to the Wairau aquifer, which is used for irrigation and drinking water (Wöhling et al., 2018). Uncertain estimates of river loss make it harder to decide how much water can be allocated (Close et al., 2014). Existing methods often assume that water passes uniformly through the riverbed. This assumption does not hold in braided rivers (Becker, 2004; Xie et al., 2016).

Active-distributed temperature sensing (A-DTS) is one alternative. A fibre optic cable records temperature every 0.25 m along its length, while copper wires inside the same cable heat it. The cable warms less when groundwater flowing past it removes heat faster. Banks et al. (2022) buried such a cable beneath the braided Waikirikiri-Selwyn River in Canterbury and used a single survey to estimate river loss. Before repeat surveys can be used to measure how river loss changes through time, we need to know whether a change in flow can be distinguished from measurement error. This had not been tested in a natural field setting. In this study, we test it at the same site.

What we did

The cable is about 100 m long and lies about 5 m below the two main channels of the river, perpendicular to the flow. The manufacturer did not give the distance between the heating wires and the optical fibres, which enters the calculation of flow. So we cut twelve short lengths of the cable and measured that distance under a microscope: 0.7 mm.

Over 24 hours on 2 and 3 September 2022, we ran twelve surveys. Each 2-hour survey heated the cable for one hour at 15 W/m (watts per metre), and then let it cool. Over the 100 m cable, this is about 1.5 kilowatts (kW) (our arithmetic). We ran the surveys while river flow was stable, so that their spread would show the reproducibility of the method. River discharge ranged from 4.02 to 4.29 m³/s (cubic metres per second), and shallow groundwater levels changed by less than 6 mm.

We then fitted a moving instantaneous line source (MILS) model to the warming curve every 0.25 m along the cable. This model describes heat spreading from a line heater into moving groundwater. The fit gives the specific discharge, the groundwater flow rate per unit area, in metres per day (m/d). We estimated the thermal conductivity of the gravels from the first survey and held it fixed for the other eleven. Any difference between surveys is therefore attributed to a change in flow.

What we found

The median specific discharge of each survey lay between 3.5 and 4.0 m/d, and the average over the 24 hours was 3.8 m/d. That is about 3,800 litres of groundwater crossing each square metre of riverbed each day (our conversion). The spread of the medians lies within the measurement error of the method, about 6% of the specific discharge. Repeating the survey appears to reduce the uncertainty. A single survey was uncertain by up to 0.44 m/d, against 0.09 m/d for the twelve surveys together. Flow varied along the cable by more than the measurement error.

Over the 72 m of wetted riverbed, 3.8 m/d equates to a river loss of 274,000 m³ per day per kilometre of river. Data from a survey in February 2021 (Banks et al., 2022) give about 38% less, because the wetted riverbed was narrower (60 m) and the average flow lower (2.8 m/d). This difference likely reflects seasonal variation in river loss.

We are more confident in the repeatability of the flow estimates than in their absolute values. The MILS model assumes a line heat source, and the complex geometry of the cable does not match it. An effective distance between heater and fibres must therefore be assumed. Our main results use 1.8 mm, the value a sensitivity analysis of the sixth survey identified as optimal. That survey averages 3.8 m/d with 1.8 mm and 1.5 m/d with the measured 0.7 mm. Across the combinations of distance and thermal conductivity tested, the average ranged from 0.6 to 18.1 m/d. This is a thirty-fold spread, by our calculation. However, the relative error was almost the same for both distances, 6.0% with 1.8 mm and 6.1% with 0.7 mm.

Limitations

We could not quantify the skin effect, the extra warming caused by the low thermal conductivity of the cable and its immediate surroundings. The thermal conductivity from the model also combines the properties of the cable materials, the region around the cable and the gravels. Our model values were lower than laboratory measurements on core from the site. The cable's polypropylene jacket conducts heat poorly and is likely to contribute to this difference.

The model assumes vertical flow only, perpendicular to the cable. Flow beneath braided rivers is likely more complex than this. Our estimates appear to follow the shape of the riverbed. The measured flow may be recharge from the river, flow within the gravels, or discharge back to the river.

Relative comparisons between surveys remain feasible despite the uncertain effective distance. We expect that repeat surveys under different river conditions, ideally with river flow gauged at the same time, would allow the seasonal variation of recharge from braided rivers to be quantified.

Glossary

Groundwater recharge is water that enters an aquifer, here from the river above.

An aquifer is a body of permeable rock or sediment that holds and transmits groundwater.

River loss is the water a river loses through its bed to the ground beneath.

A braided river has many interconnected channels separated by gravel bars.

Active-distributed temperature sensing (A-DTS) measures temperature along a fibre optic cable while the cable is heated.

Specific discharge is the volume of groundwater crossing a unit area per unit time, in metres per day (m/d).

Thermal conductivity is a measure of how readily a material conducts heat.

The MILS model is a mathematical solution for the temperature around a line heat source in moving groundwater.

The skin effect is the temperature rise caused by the low thermal conductivity of the cable and the material immediately around it.

Wetted riverbed is the part of the riverbed under water, here 72 m wide along the cable.

A watt per metre (W/m) is the heating power applied to each metre of cable.

Abstract

Braided rivers are a major contributor of groundwater recharge, yet little is known about how recharge rates vary in time. Existing methods for estimating groundwater recharge from rivers (i.e., river loss) are inadequate for studying highly heterogeneous braided river systems at a sufficient spatiotemporal resolution. To do so, active-distributed temperature sensing (A-DTS) is employed, which combines fibre optic temperature measurements with an active heat source, enabling high-resolution quantification of water fluxes. In this study, twelve successive A-DTS surveys were conducted during a 24-hour experiment on a 100 m horizontal subsurface hybrid fibre optic cable installed at 5 m depth beneath a braided river. The experiment was carried out under conditions where the river stage and flow were relatively stable to demonstrate the reproducibility and effectiveness of the A-DTS method for measuring groundwater specific discharge. This foundational work will provide a high level of confidence in the method for future studies aimed at evaluating temporal variations in groundwater recharge. The median groundwater specific discharge values calculated over the 24-hour period had a very narrow range from 3.5 to 4.0 m/d across the wetted footprint of the river, which is within the measurement error of the installation (6 %), indicating relatively stable groundwater recharge during the experiment. This provides confidence in the repeatability of the A-DTS method as an effective technology for quantifying river loss over longer time periods, to understand seasonal variability of groundwater recharge in braided river systems.
