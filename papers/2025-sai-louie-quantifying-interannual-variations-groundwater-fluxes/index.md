## In brief

Over nearly three years we used heated fibre optic cables to measure groundwater flow beneath a braided river in Canterbury, New Zealand. River flow varied about tenfold between surveys, but average groundwater flux remained between 2.4 and 5.9 m/day. Riverbed changes after a flood likely had the largest effect.

- Over nearly three years, 28 surveys gave average specific discharge of 3.5 to 5.9 m/day beneath the main channel and 2.4 to 4.5 m/day beneath the secondary channel.
- Specific discharge had a negligible relationship with river flow beneath the main channel and a moderate negative one beneath the secondary channel.
- The surveys suggest no strong seasonal pattern in specific discharge, although only two surveys were in summer.
- After the May 2021 flood, average specific discharge rose 30.5% beneath the main channel, most under its margins, and fell 17.6% beneath the secondary channel.
- The rise beneath the margins of the main channel is likely related to thinning of the sediment above the cable there after the flood.

## The problem

Recharge from braided rivers helps sustain groundwater resources and river
ecosystems around the world (Coluccio and Morgan, 2019). In the Canterbury Plains of New Zealand,
braided rivers are estimated to contribute more than half of the surface water
flow (Canterbury Water, 2009).

Between a braided river and the deeper regional aquifer is a shallow layer of
gravel, reworked by floods, that stores river water. Wilson et al. (2024) called
this layer a braidplain aquifer (BPA). Flow between the river channels and the
BPA is called parafluvial flow. Most methods for measuring exchange between a
river and a shallow aquifer give an estimate at a single point, or over a longer
distance at a single time (Coluccio and Morgan, 2019). Our goal in this study
was to quantify how parafluvial flow changes over several years, as river flow
and the shape of the riverbed change.

## What we did

Our site is a 1 km reach of the upper Waikirikiri Selwyn River in Canterbury.
Two 100 m fibre optic cables had been installed across the river by horizontal
directional drilling (HDD) (Banks et al., 2022). One cable, HDD1, passes beneath the
main channel, and the other, HDD2, beneath a smaller secondary channel. They
are buried at depths of 0.56 to 5.55 m.

We heated copper conductors in each cable at 15 watts per metre of cable for 20
minutes. From late October 2021 we extended this to 1 hour. A distributed temperature
sensing (DTS) instrument recorded the temperature along the optical fibre every
0.25 m. We then fitted a model of heat transfer by conduction and advection
(transport by moving water) to the measured temperatures. This gave the
specific discharge, the volume of groundwater crossing each square metre of
aquifer per day, expressed in metres per day. The method gives the magnitude of the
flux. We used only the saturated sections of each cable.
An earlier study at the site put the uncertainty of a single survey at 6% (Sai
Louie et al., 2024). Between November 2020 and August 2023 we ran 28 surveys,
14 on each cable, at roughly monthly intervals.

## What we found

River flow at a gauge 9.5 km upstream ranged from 0.67 to 225 m³/s (cubic
metres per second) over the study. A flood on 31 May 2021 peaked at 225 m³/s,
about 90 times the median flow of 2.51 m³/s. The main channel moved south and
became about 11 m wider. Several mature willow trees that grew on the bank were
in the middle of the channel after the flood. Because surveys were not possible during floods,
river flow at survey times varied by about one order of magnitude.

Averaged over the saturated part of each cable, specific discharge ranged from
3.5 to 5.9 m/day on HDD1 and from 2.4 to 4.5 m/day on HDD2. The average over
both cables was 4.2 m/day. By our arithmetic, this is 4,200 litres of water
crossing each square metre of aquifer each day. The interquartile range (the
spread of the middle half of the surveys) was 20.4% of the median on HDD1 and
13.3% on HDD2. Specific discharge showed a negligible relationship with river
flow on HDD1 and a moderate negative relationship on HDD2. The results suggest
no strong seasonal influence, although we ran only two surveys in summer.

Changes to the riverbed after the May 2021 flood likely had the largest effect
on specific discharge. On HDD1, the average after the flood was 30.5% higher than
before. The first survey on HDD1 after the flood, about five weeks later, showed
an increase under the left margin of the main channel. The next survey showed
an increase under the right margin. Specific discharge beneath both margins
remained above its pre-flood level for the rest of the study, although it fell in
the last four or five surveys.

After the flood, the sediment above HDD1 was thinner at the margins of the main
channel. The increases in specific discharge are likely related to this
thinning. When the main channel moved, it also appears to have connected to a
remnant braid. We suggest this contributed to the increase under the left
margin. The secondary channel above HDD2 changed little in shape, yet on HDD2
the average fell by 17.6% after the flood. This fall could be due to
the secondary channel switching from losing water to the aquifer to gaining
water from it. However, we could not verify this. The flood destroyed the river
monitoring equipment.

## Limitations

Our method treats the flux as one-dimensional and does not resolve its
direction. In reality, flow beneath the river is multidirectional. A numerical model of the site puts the horizontal flux at
about an order of magnitude greater than the vertical flux (Di Ciacca et al.,
2024). Our estimates are therefore likely dominated by horizontal flow. The
parts inside the cable are not arranged symmetrically about its centre. This likely affects our estimates by an amount
that we have not quantified. It may bias the absolute values of specific discharge,
but the method can still identify changes along the cable and over time.

We measured at one site. At sites with a different geomorphology or flood
history, the processes we identified may be stronger, weaker or absent. Horizontal cables beneath a river are costly to
install. Vertical or steeply inclined cables would cost less and could help extend
these findings to a whole river reach.

## Glossary

A **braided river** has several active channels on a mobile bed of gravel.

A **braidplain aquifer (BPA)** is a shallow layer of flood-reworked gravel
beneath and beside a braided river that stores river water.

An **aquifer** is a body of rock or sediment that stores groundwater and lets it
flow.

**Recharge** is water that enters an aquifer, here from a river.

**Parafluvial flow** is the flow of water between a river channel and the BPA,
through the sediment beside and beneath the channels.

**Specific discharge** is the volume of groundwater flowing through each square
metre of aquifer per unit time, in metres per day.

**Distributed temperature sensing (DTS)** measures temperature at closely
spaced points along a fibre optic cable.

**Horizontal directional drilling** is a method of drilling a near-horizontal
hole underground, here beneath a river, to install a cable.

**Conduction** is the transfer of heat through a material, and **advection** is
the transport of heat by moving water.

A **saturated** sediment has its pores completely filled with water.

The **margins** of a channel are the zones beneath its left and right banks,
looking downstream.

A **remnant braid** is a former river channel that is no longer active.

A **losing** channel loses water to the aquifer beneath it, and a **gaining**
channel receives water from it.

The **median** is the middle value of a set, and the **interquartile range** is
the spread of the middle half of the values.

An **order of magnitude** is a factor of ten.

## Abstract

Braided rivers are a significant contributor to groundwater flow in braidplain aquifer (BPA) systems, yet the spatiotemporal variations in flow exchange remain poorly understood. This study presents an extensive active‐distributed temperature sensing data set collected over a 3‐year period from two 100‐m long fiber optic cables installed up to ∼5 m depth below a braided river channel in New Zealand to quantify specific discharge (i.e., parafluvial flow) within the BPA at high spatial resolution. Despite river flow fluctuating by two orders of magnitude, specific discharge within the BPA showed moderate variation, with values ranging from 3.5 to 5.9 m d−1 on HDD1 (20.4% variance relative to the median) and from 2.4 to 4.5 m d−1 on HDD2 (13.3% variance relative to the median) over the study period. Geomorphology influences hydraulic conductivity, thereby affecting the hydraulic gradient. Following a flood event, specific discharge increased beneath the river margins over time, with the largest increase corresponding with areas of greatest geomorphic change, and changes to the subsurface flow dynamics. Surveys conducted after the flood event, where river flow peaked at 225 m3 s−1 (compared to a median of 2.51 m3 s−1), showed a marked increase in specific discharge. These subsurface changes occurred gradually, with no obvious seasonal influence, compared to rapid geomorphic surface alterations. This translates to variable specific discharge within the BPA in both space and time caused by changing bedform and connectivity to preferential flowpaths. Geomorphic changes following a flood event had the largest impact on specific discharge.
