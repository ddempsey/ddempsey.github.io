- 10 **Abstract** 

- 11 Tectonic extension applied to the upper crust commonly results in the development of 

- 12 sub-parallel arrays of faults and fault belts. A computational model of an extending 

- 13 elastic-viscous-plastic crust is presented, which describes the evolution of stress, strain 

- 14 and displacement in the vicinity of an active normal fault. Within the upper crust discrete 

- 15 episodes of fault slip accommodate up to half the applied extension rate, with the balance 

- 16 accommodated by bands of frictional plastic shearing representing young proto-faults. 

- 17 Repeated coseismic shear stress reductions in the near-fault vicinity impose a stress- 

- 18 failure shadow where the formation of new faults is prohibited. A near-surface 

- 19 compressional stress regime in the footwall causes the failure shadow to extend 

- 20 disproportionally into this fault block, thereby favoring the formation of new faults in the 

- 21 hanging wall. A continuous shallowing of fault dip is also documented and supports a

- 28 **1. Introduction** 

- 29 Extension of the lithosphere in rifting basins is accommodated through displacement on 

- 30 normal dip-slip faults or by the emplacement of dikes, i.e., intrusive magmatism. Such 

- 31 structures are observed at rifting at both convergent (Taymaz et al., 1991; Wilson et al., 

- 32 1995) and divergent plate margins (Ebinger, 1989), and intra-plate settings (Illies, 1972; 

- 33 Puskas et al., 2007). Over time the character of normal faulting evolves, with individual 

- 34 fault strands organizing into sub-parallel en-echelon arrays and, with continued linkage, 

- 35 into large fault belts (Cowie et al., 2005). However, fault planes may also become 

- 36 inactive (Jackson, 1999). Extension can cause fault planes to rotate about a horizontal 

- 37 axis, shallowing the dip angle to a point where continued reactivation of the fault 

- 38 becomes unlikely (Sibson, 1985). In addition, stresses generated by an existing fault have 

- 39 been hypothesized (Goldsworthy and Jackson, 2001) to induce a migration of faulting in 

- 40 a basinward direction (Dart et al., 1995; Horton and Schmitt, 1998; Goldsworthy and 

- 41 Jackson, 2001; Sanchez et al., 2010). 

- 42 

- 43 During discrete episodes of dip-slip on normal faults, extension is accommodated by 

- 44 translation of one fault block relative to the other. Displacement is primarily parallel to 

- 45 the fault plane, and therefore horizontal movements are accompanied by vertical 

- 46 displacements. This behavior translates to uplift of the footwall block and a 

- 47 comparatively larger subsidence of the hanging wall (e.g., Barr, 1987). Conceptual 

- 48 models employ lithospheric flexure (Weissel and Karner, 1989; Kusznir et al., 1991), 

- 49 domino rotation (Wernicke and Burchfiel, 1982), or isostatic rebound (Barr, 1987; 

- 50 Westaway, 1992) on either planar or listric fault surfaces to describe this deformation. 

- 51 However, field studies indicate that fault slip aggregated across a rift does not always 

- 52 account for the full geodetic extension rate (e.g., Villamor and Berryman, 2001; Wallace 

- 53 et al., 2004). Additional deformation mechanisms or movements on undiscovered faults 

- 54 must account for the shortfall. 

- 55 

- 56 Normal fault systems evolve and grow over many thousands of years in response to the 

- 57 continuous application of far-field tectonic extension (e.g., Villamor and Berryman, 

- 58 2001; Bell et al., 2009). Such evolution involves the initiation of new faults (Jackson and 

- 59 McKenzie, 1983), which is itself an indication that extension is not completely 

- 60 accommodated by existing structures. Potential causes of this shortfall include: a 

- 61 migration of the locus of extension away from the faulted region; a suspension of activity 

- 62 on pre-existing faults due to fault rotation (Jackson, 1999), restrengthening of the fault 

- 63 (Li et al., 2003; Muhuri et al., 2003) or stress shadowing by other faults (Cowie 1998); 

- 64 distributed frictional deformation (see section 2, below); or a suspension of alternative 

- 65 mechanisms of extension accommodation, e.g., dike emplacement (Rowland et al., 2010). 

- 66 

- 67 The purpose of this work is to investigate specifically the role that frictional plasticity 

- 68 plays in the evolution of a normal fault system. From a theoretical perspective, frictional 

- 69 plasticity is the non-recoverable deformation of the crust through frictional sliding on 

- 70 hypothetical fracture planes. Such planes are presumed to exist abundantly throughout the 

- 71 crust (Ranalli and Murphy, 1987) at orientations that are optimal for reactivation within 

- 72 the prevailing stress field. Within the crust, these structural discontinuities may represent 

- 73 joints, dilational jogs, bedding planes or extensional shear fractures (Sibson, 1996; 2000) 

- 74 across which frictional shear displacements might be expected to occur. Coalescence of a 

- 75 multitude of these shear offset structures within a well constrained deformation band may 

- 76 be a central process leading to the formation of a new fault (e.g., Pollard and Fletcher, 

- 77 2005). Thus, an investigation of frictional plasticity, treated as a precursor to fault 

- 78 formation, can address where subsequent faults should localize and provide insight into 

- 79 fault zone evolution. 

- 80 

- 81 We build on earlier models of Ellis et al. (2006) and Nüchter and Ellis (2010) that 

- 82 prescribe fault surfaces directly and model frictional sliding between fault blocks over 

- 83 many seismic cycles. We model the evolution of normal fault systems shortly after the 

- 84 onset of rifting. Modeled deformation includes: discrete episodes of slip between fault 

- 85 blocks that displace independently along the plane of contact; frictional plastic yield 

- 86 within the brittle upper crust; and viscous flow in the lower crust. The relative importance 

- 87 of each mechanism in accommodating the regional extension rate indicates the likelihood 

- 88 of development of additional fault structures and the positions of these relative to the 

- 89 primary fault. We observe a gradual shallowing of the dip angle of the fault plane due to 

- 90 rotation, which is a prerequisite for the development of cross-cutting faults (Profett, 1977; 

- 91 Nur et al., 1986) as older structures rotate beyond the limit for reactivation (Sibson, 

- 92 1985). 

- 93 

- 94 Results from the computational model are interpreted in the context of the western Gulf 

- 95 of Corinth, an asymmetric graben in mainland Greece, comprised of several major north- 

- 96 dipping faults accommodating extension of 12-14 mm yr<sup>-1</sup> (e.g., Clarke et al., 1997; 

- 97 Briole et al., 2000; Floyd et al., 2010). Geomorphological and stratigraphic evidence 

- 98 indicate a migration of faulting in a basinward direction (Goldsworthy and Jackson, 

- 99 2001; Flotté et al., 2005; Rohais et al., 2007) as new faults developed in the hanging 

- 100 walls of older structures (Jackson, 1999). Flotté et al. (2005) propose a tectonic model 

- 101 utilizing a low-angle detachment fault underlying steeper surface faults, although this 

- 102 model is not universally accepted and is not applied by us (e.g., McNeill et al., 2005). In 

- 103 this paper, we propose a model for basinward fault migration based on modeled 

- 104 distributions of stress and frictional plasticity in the vicinity of existing faults.

- 106 **2. Previous numerical experiments** 

- 107 Computational modeling of fault systems on short timescales has been used to examine 

- 108 rupture propagation along a fault under rate-state friction constraints (Dieterich, 1979; 

- 109 Ruina, 1983), rupture propagation in an asymmetric geometry (Oglesby et al., 2000), and 

- 110 near-fault coseismic deformation of an elastic crust (e.g., Freund and Barnett, 1976; 

- 111 Gupta and Scholz, 1998). Such studies constrain the dynamics of fault rupture and are 

- 112 useful in modeling ground acceleration and quasi-static coseismic displacements; 

- 113 however, they do not account for deformation in fault systems over many seismic cycles. 

- 114 

- 115 Over longer timescales, numerical models of fault architecture and rift evolution, such as 

- 116 an extension to the propagator matrix method of Rundle (1980) by King et al. (1988) and 

- 117 Stein et al. (1988), have explored topographical development of normal faults over 

- 118 multiple earthquake cycles and in continual depositional and erosional processes. Their 

- 119 model indicates that the degree of lithospheric flexure and the width of the deformation 

- 120 region are controlled by the effective elastic thickness of the crust. Finite element models 

- 121 have expanded on these findings, investigating the effects of finite elastic strength in the 

- 122 upper crust, and tracking patterns of failure in the vicinity of a fault (e.g., Melosh and 

- 123 Williams, 1989; Bott, 1997). Plastic deformation reduces the effective elastic thickness of 

- 124 the crust, thereby controlling graben dimension by migrating the outer flexure hinge 

- 125 closer to the fault scarp (Bott, 1997). 

- 126 

- 127 Melosh and Williams (1989) detailed a finite element model that accommodates slip 

- 128 between 65° dipping conjugate elements and which tracks proximity to Mohr-Coulomb 

- 129 failure in the vicinity of a dip-slip fault. Their analysis indicates a maximum propensity 

- 130 to failure in the near surface of the hanging wall, and also at the fault tip where the 

- 131 maximum gradient in dip-slip is encountered. On the basis of strain-energy minimization, 

- 132 they argue that antithetic faults should form in the hanging wall in response to coseismic 

- 133 stress changes caused by rupture of the main fault. This result, which is specific to 

- 134 systems in which the main fault dips at 65°, does not account for coupling between 

- 135 gravitational potential and strain energy (Dempsey et al., 2012). 

- 136 

- 137 Henk (2006) uses a thermo-mechanical model with a viscoelastic-plastic rheology to 

- 138 investigate lithospheric extension and deformation about a reactivated thrust fault, for a 

- 139 variety of strength parameters and strain rates. These models also demonstrate strain 

- 140 localization in the near-surface of the hanging wall, interpreted as a crestal collapse 

- 141 graben (McClay and Ellis, 1987). Others have used 3-dimensional thermo-mechanical 

- 142 models (Braun et al., 2008; Popov and Sobolev, 2008) to model rifting over large spatial 

- 143 and temporal scales (100's of km and millions of years), and have reproduced rift 

- 144 bounding faults through bands of localized strain (Huismans et al., 2005).

- 146 **3. Numerical model** 

- 147 The finite element method, implemented here using the software suite Abaqus/Standard 

- 148 (http://www.simulia.com/products/abaqus_standard.html), is used to solve deformation 

- 149 and stress equations for an extending crust. Far-field boundary conditions and a cyclical 

- 150 fault-friction regime drive the evolution of stress and deformation in time. This approach 

- 151 was previously used by Ellis et al. (2006) and Nüchter and Ellis (2010) to develop 2-D 

- 152 models of fault systems in contractional and extensional tectonic settings. 

- 153 

- 154 We consider deformation parallel to the strike of a dip-slip fault as negligible, which 

- 155 allows us to model a 2-dimensional slice of the crust under plane strain conditions. This 

- 156 slice is oriented perpendicular to the axis of a generic rift system and measures 60 km 

- 157 horizontally and 25 km vertically. Such dimensions are presumed to be sufficient to 

- 158 represent the relatively shallow seismogenic zone in thermally weakened rifts 

- 159 (approximately 8 km, e.g., Bibby et al. (1995)) and isolate fault behavior from the 

- 160 influence of side and lower boundaries. 

- 161

- 163 parameterized by Young's Modulus, _E_ , and Poisson's ratio, ν. For a complete summary of

- 214 Deformation of the crust by tectonic extension occurs due to velocity boundary 

- 215 conditions imposed at the lateral boundaries. GPS investigations of the western Gulf of 

- 216 Corinth indicate that extension is on the order of 12-14 mm yr<sup>-1</sup> (e.g., Clarke et al., 1997; 

- 217 Briole et al., 2000; Floyd et al., 2010). In the models detailed here, we apply a horizontal 

- 218 velocity boundary condition of -6 and +6 mm yr<sup>-1</sup> to the left and right boundaries, 

- 219 respectively, to account for this forcing (see Figure 1(a)). This differs from previous 

- 220 models (Bott, 1997; Ellis et al., 2006; Nüchter and Ellis, 2010) where one boundary is 

- 221 constrained by a `no displacement' condition and the full tectonic forcing applied to the 

- 222 other boundary. The lower boundary is fixed at zero vertical displacement and the upper 

- 223 surface is unconstrained. Gravity is applied throughout the model as a body force. A 

- 224 constant density of 2800 kg m<sup>-3</sup> is applied throughout the crust.

- 230 to model normal fault earthquakes in the Gulf of Corinth. The fault is prescribed as either 

- 231 `locked', where the surfaces are prevented from sliding against one another, or `slipping', 

- 232 where frictional sliding according to Amonton's law is permitted on the interface

- 247 An initial stress state for the model is evolved by applying the velocity boundary 

- 248 conditions over geological time (30 kyr, see Figure 1(d)). This stress ‘spin-up’ (Hetland 

- 249 and Hager, 2006; Ellis et al., 2006) phase produces a lithostatic vertical stress, σ _z_ , and a 

- 250 horizontal stress, σ _x_ , such that the differential stress is equal to the lower of _qp_ or _qv_ , i.e., a 

- 251 critically stressed crust at frictional plastic yield at shallow depths and viscous yield 

- 252 below the BDT (Townend and Zoback, 2000). Deformation that occurs during the stress 

- 253 spin-up period is discarded at the beginning of a new simulation, while the stress state is 

- 254 retained as an initial condition. The initial stress state, characterized by the differential 

- 255 stress (and equivalent to the Von Mises stress under plane strain conditions), is shown as 

- 256 a function of depth in Figure 1(c).

- 258 The seismic cycle, in which long periods of fault dormancy are punctuated by short, 

- 259 violent episodes of fault slip and followed by a readjustment period of afterslip and 

- 260 aftershock activity, is approximated in the model by a two-step cycle. The interseismic 

- 261 period refers to the interval between earthquakes in which extension applied at the model 

- 262 boundaries alters the displacement and stress distribution within the model domain. Each 

- 263 interseismic period is followed by a seismic period, in which the friction coefficient on 

- 264 the fault surface is substantially lowered and displacements of the fault blocks are driven 

- 265 by an imbalance between gravitational and elastic forces. The model reaches dynamic 

- 266 equilibrium, where elastic changes accumulated during the interseismic period are offset 

- 267 by those of the seismic period, after 10 seismic cycles. In this paper we analyze results 

- 268 from the 12<sup>th</sup> cycle (Figure 1(d)). Displacements of the model boundaries caused by the 

- 269 velocity boundary conditions are negligible during the 60 second seismic period. Any slip 

- 270 that occurs during this period is due to stress imbalances accumulated during the 

- 271 interseismic period. Fault slip that occurs during the seismic period includes all possible 

- 272 afterslip.

- 274 **4. The seismic cycle** 

- 275 Figure 2 summarizes salient aspects of the modeled seismic cycle including (a) coseismic 

- 276 surface displacements, (b) fault parallel displacement that occurs during the 12<sup>th</sup> fault 

- 277 rupture, (c) the distribution of the differential stress after 12 seismic cycles, (d) elastic 

- 278 strain accumulated during the 12<sup>th</sup> fault rupture, and the total accumulated (e) viscous and 

- 279 (f) frictional-plastic strain after 12 seismic cycles.

- 281 A sudden decrease in friction on the fault surface during the seismic period permits 

- 282 displacement of the fault blocks relative to one another. As the problem is modeled under 

- 283 plane-strain conditions, along-strike displacement is not considered and the resulting 

- 284 motions are approximately parallel to the dip of the fault. Figure 2(b) plots the fault- 

- 285 parallel displacement field of the crust during the seismic period, in which “up-dip” is 

- 286 taken as the positive direction. Maximum displacements are observed at the fault scarp 

- 287 and decay with distance, consistent with geodetic evidence of large normal fault ruptures 

- 288 at Hegben Lake, Montana and Borah Peak, Idaho (Barrientos et al., 1987). Along the 

- 289 fault plane the footwall exhibits 0.6 m of upward-directed fault-parallel displacement 

- 290 from the surface to 5 km depth (Figure 2(b) inset). Similarly the hanging wall is displaced 

- 291 a constant 0.9 m in a down-dip direction for the same range of depths. The total single- 

- 292 event offset is therefore 1.5 m, which consistent with the order of magnitude of historical 

- 293 ruptures in the Gulf of Corinth (Koukouvelas and Doutsos, 1996; Roberts, 1996). In 

- 294 addition to material (Gudmundsson, 2004) and fault geometry (Section 4) considerations, 

- 295 the length of the loading period preceding an earthquake, i.e., the interseismic period, 

- 296 exercises an important control on the size of the subsequent fault rupture. In these 

- 297 simulations we have selected an interseismic period of 220 years that produces fault 

- 298 ruptures of an appropriate size for the region under consideration. Fault slip decays from 

- 299 its maximum value to zero over a depth interval of 5.0-8.5 km, below which viscous 

- 300 deformation maintains a stress state that inhibits slip. The true depth of the modeled fault 

- 301 is therefore determined by material parameters and the applied strain rate and is not a 

- 302 prescribed feature of the geometry.

- 304 The distribution of the differential stress (Figure 2(c)) reflects the brittle and viscous 

- 305 rheologies prevailing in the upper and lower crust, respectively (see Figure 1(c) and Eq. 

- 306 (2) and (4)). The maximum differential stress, marking the transition from brittle to 

- 307 ductile behavior, occurs at a depth of 6 km. Perturbations to the profile in Figure 1(c) are 

- 308 observed close to the fault surface and are caused by coseismic movements of the fault 

- 309 blocks and gradients in slip at the fault tip. In the hanging wall, a broad maximum in _q_ is 

- 310 centered on the BDT, while in the adjacent footwall there is a corresponding reduction 

- 311 compared to background levels for that depth. 

- 312 

- 313 Viscous deformation is the dominant contribution to deformation at the high temperatures 

- 314 and pressures found in the lower crust (>9 km depth). Frictional plastic deformation or 

- 315 slip on the fault surface are absent from this region of the crust so that, during the 

- 316 interseismic period, 2.64 m (12 mm yr<sup>-1</sup> × 220 yr) of tectonic extension must be 

- 317 accommodated by viscous strain. As the strain rate,  _v_ , is proportional to _q4_ (see Table 1 

- 318 and Eq. (3) with _n_ = 4), the additional accumulation of εv in Figure 2(e) along the fault 

- 319 root at depths >6 km points to an increase in the stress above the background level for 

- 320 this depth. This increase, evident in Figure 2(c), is attributed to the upper crustal 

- 321 displacements of the fault blocks and the accommodation of these motions within the 

- 322 mid-crust. The extra viscous straining directly beneath the fault alters post-seismic 

- 323 deformation at the surface (Ellis et al., 2006) and helps restore the stress state on the fault 

- 324 to its equilibrium state (Nüchter and Ellis, 2010). 

- 325 

- 326 Frictional-plastic strain accumulates exclusively in the upper crust at depths shallower 

- 327 than 6 km and localizes in shear bands that dip at approximately 45° from the horizontal, 

- 328 as discussed in section 2. Qualitatively, frictional-plastic strain occurs preferentially in a 

- 329 triangular wedge of the hanging wall block and is conspicuously absent from much of the 

- 330 footwall. Regions of increased plastic strain are also observed at the lateral boundaries of 

- 331 the model; however, these regions are outside the influence of the fault and represent 

- 332 background strain behavior. The presence of multiple shear bands, as opposed to one or 

- 333 two bands of high strain, is consistent with the “distributed” mode of fracturing described 

- 334 by (Schueller et al., 2010). They showed that if the applied extension rate cannot be 

- 335 accommodated by displacements on existing frictional plastic shear zones, which in turn 

- 336 are limited by the slip velocity in both brittle and ductile media, then new shear zones 

- 337 form to accommodate the shortfall. However, with further deformation such that these 

- 338 shear bands experience strain softening due to accumulated plastic strain, we expect that 

- 339 displacement would localize onto fewer structures.

- 341 **5. Extension budget** 

- 342 The extension rate of the modeled rift, which is applied as a velocity boundary condition 

- 343 at the lateral boundaries, must be accommodated within the crust by some combination of 

- 344 the available means of deformation, i.e., by fault slip, or the accumulation of elastic, 

- 345 frictional plastic, or viscous strain. The relative contribution of each to the total extension 

- 346 of the crust during a single seismic cycle comprises the extension budget. For instance, if 

- 347 during a single seismic cycle tectonic extension of 2.64 m is comprised of 0.66 m of 

- 348 horizontal elastic strain, 0.66 m of horizontal frictional-plastic strain, and 1.32 m 

- 349 horizontal component of fault slip, then the relative contribution of each component is 

- 350 0.25, 0.25 and 0.5, respectively. Depending on the means of deformation available, the 

- 351 extension budget will vary with both depth (e.g., viscous strain does not accumulate in 

- 352 the upper crust) and time (e.g., fault slip occurs only during the seismic period). Figure 3 

- 353 shows a representative extension budget with depth for the (a) interseismic and (b) 

- 354 seismic periods, and (c) a full seismic cycle, where each component is normalized against 

- 355 the total extension (2.64 m) during the full cycle. 

- 356 

- 357 Figure 3(a) indicates that viscous strain is the sole contributor to deformation at depths 

- 358 greater than 9 km and, due to its slow accumulation rate, occurs principally during the 

- 359 interseismic period. Small changes in elastic strain are also observed at these depths, 

- 360 although, summed over the interseismic and seismic periods, their net effect is nearly 

- 361 zero, which is characteristic of the dynamic equilibrium of the system. 

- 362 

- 363 Figure 3(c-d) indicates that the presence of a rupturing fault permits extension of the 

- 364 upper crust to be distributed between the two brittle failure mechanisms of fault slip and 

- 365 frictional plastic deformation. However, the accumulation of each component is mutually 

- 366 exclusive, i.e., fault slip occurs rapidly during the seismic period (Figure 3(b)) whereas 

- 367 frictional-plastic strain accumulates slowly during the interseismic period (Figure 3(a)). 

- 368 Coseismic frictional-plastic strain accumulation is significant for steeper dipping faults 

- 369 (see Section 6). A significant component of transient elastic strain accommodates 

- 370 extension during the interseismic period, but is replaced by fault slip in the following 

- 371 seismic period. It also causes a reduction of the maximum shear stress, (σ1 – σ3)/2, 

- 372 throughout the crust wherever the least principal compressive stress is horizontal, i.e., 

- 373 σ3=σ _xx_ . Accumulation of frictional-plastic strain ceases for a brief post-seismic period 

- 374 until extension restores the preseismic stress state to frictional yield (Dempsey et al., 375 2012). 

- 376 

- 377 Figure 3(a) and (c) show that the transition from purely brittle deformation, including 

- 378 fault slip and frictional-plastic straining, to purely viscous deformation occurs over a 5.0- 

- 379 9.0 km depth interval. Frictional plastic deformation terminates over a shallower 5.0-6.5 

- 380 km depth interval, with a small overlap between the brittle and viscous rheologies 

- 381 indicative of the exponential decrease in viscous strain rate with decreasing temperatures 

- 382 (Equation (3)). Fault slip penetrates further into the brittle-ductile transition zone, 

- 383 terminating over depths of 6.5-9.0 km. This bimodal truncation of the two frictional 

- 384 behaviors is due to the relative frictional weakness of the fault (μ=0.2 compared with the 

- 385 intact crust μ=tan(φ’)≈0.3) and indicates a mixed brittle-viscous region between a purely 

- 386 brittle upper crust and purely viscous lower crust. In this region weakened, embrittled 

- 387 faults are able to penetrate the mostly viscous lower crust (Scholz, 1988, 1998), while 

- 388 frictional plasticity is limited to shallower depths. 

- 389 

- 390 Figure 4(a) considers the composition of the full cycle extension budget within the upper 

- 391 crust (< 5.0 km depth) as a function of the loading period that precedes an earthquake. 

- 392 For this analysis each simulation was “spun-up” to the same dynamic stress equilibrium, 

- 393 using a nominal interseismic period of 220 years, and followed by a single seismic cycle 

- 394 at the modified length. Thus, Figure 4(a) considers the effect of seismic cycle length 

- 395 independent of the previous rupture history. It also illustrates extension accommodation 

- 396 in the upper crust at different times during the interseismic period. As in Figure 3, 

- 397 components were normalized against the total extension applied over the full cycle. For 

- 398 longer seismic cycles, distributed frictional-plastic strain accounts for a proportionally 

- 399 larger share, e.g., approaching 50% of the total extension for an interseismic period 220 

- 400 years in length. During the early stages of the interseismic quiescence following fault 

- 401 rupture, tectonic extension is mostly taken up by the accumulation of elastic strain 

- 402 throughout the upper crust, which is later exchanged for fault slip. Extension reduces 

- 403 horizontal stress, σ _xx_ , which causes the differential stress to approach the limit of 

- 404 frictional plastic failure, _qp_ , and switches large areas of the crust from elastic to frictional 

- 405 plastic behavior. Elastic strain is temporarily saturated in these regions, and subsequent 

- 406 accumulated strain is frictional plastic. Each successive increment of extension during the 

- 407 interseismic period then has two compounding effects: (i) to effect stress changes that 

- 408 switch ever larger regions of the crust from elastic to frictional plastic deformation and 

- 409 (ii) to accumulate frictional-plastic strain within an increasing volume of yielding crust 

- 410 and elastic strain within a decreasing volume of elastic crust. Horizontal stress is 

- 411 increased in the subsequent earthquake step due to coseismic displacements of the fault 

- 412 blocks. 

- 413 

- 414 Fault dip also exerts control on the composition of the upper crust extension budget (see 

- 415 Figure 4(b)). For a system in dynamic equilibrium with a seismic cycle 220 years in 

- 416 length, a shallow fault, θ = 40-50°, accommodates more than half of the applied 

- 417 extension through fault slip, with the shortfall accommodated by frictional plasticity. 

- 418 However, steeper faults accommodate a decreasing proportion of the total extension 

- 419 through displacement of the fault blocks. This decrease is because the production of a 

- 420 given amount of horizontal extension requires an increasing component of vertical 

- 421 movement, and thus a correspondingly larger earthquake, at steeper angles of fault dip. A 

- 422 reduction in the fault-slip component of the extension budget must be compensated by an 

- 423 increase in other components, which for an upper crust at dynamic equilibrium must be 

- 424 frictional-plastic strain. This result is largely independent of the elastic properties of the 

- 425 crust, and the partitioning of upper crustal extension between the accumulation of 

- 426 frictional-plastic strain and episodic fault slip appears to be controlled principally by the 

- 427 geometry of the system.

- 429 **6. Distribution of frictional plasticity** 

- 430 By definition, frictional-plastic strain accumulates in those regions where the differential 

- 431 (plane-strain Von Mises) stress exceeds the threshold for failure, i.e., _q>qp_ . The 

- 432 accumulation of frictional-plastic strain then, to some extent, reflects the stress state of 

- 433 the crust. Figure 5(a-b) plots the proximity of the differential stress to the limit of 

- 434 frictional plastic yield, i.e., _qp-q_ , to a depth of 8 km, prior to and following fault rupture. 

- 435 The frictional plastic yield limit is pressure dependent and must therefore be recalculated 

- 436 following fault rupture to account for changes in mean stress. The sharp transition from 

- 437 brittle to ductile failure is evident at a depth of 6 km, consistent with the thickness of the 

- 438 frictional plastic layer in the extension budget (see section 5). Below 6 km, viscous 

- 439 deformation maintains low stresses and ensures that _q<qp_ . In the brittle upper crust and 

- 440 prior to fault rupture, the majority of the hanging-wall crust is either at or within 0.1 MPa 

- 441 of failure. Coseismic stress changes generally decrease the proximity to failure by 

- 442 between 0.1 and 1 MPa. 

- 443 

- 444 Repeated fault slip and a small dynamic friction coefficient maintain the near-fault 

- 445 differential stress at up to 10 MPa below failure, creating a stress shadow. Under plane 

- 446 strain conditions, the Von Mises stress is twice the maximum shear stress, and thus its 

- 447 reduction is due to the repeated coseismic relief of shear stress on the fault plane. Shear 

- 448 stress drops during the modeled episodes of fault slip are approximately 0.5-1 MPa, 

- 449 which indicates that the magnitude of the stress-failure shadow varies by up to 10% over 

- 450 the seismic period. Greater stress drops associated with larger earthquakes and longer 

- 451 recurrence intervals are then permitted for this fault, but the shear stress in the near-fault 

- 452 region cannot increase above _qp_ . If the differential stress is consistently restored (through 

- 453 a drop in stress) to its postseismic value in Figure 5(b) for any recurrence interval, then it 

- 454 follows that stress drop, and thus earthquake magnitude (Hanks and Kanamori, 1979), are 

- 455 limited by the frictional strength of the upper crust. 

- 456 

- 457 Upward-directed fault slip along the footwall scarp during an earthquake causes an 

- 458 increase in elevation at the surface, but also a component of horizontal displacement 

- 459 away from the fault plane. During the brief seismic slip-events displacements at the side 

- 460 boundaries of the model are approximately zero, and thus coseismic fault displacements 

- 461 result in a horizontal compression of the crust. This elastic deformation lowers the 

- 462 differential stress in the near surface of the footwall and at depths less than 500 m, the 

- 463 compression is so great that the horizontal stress exceeds the vertical, i.e., |σ _xx_ |>|σ _zz_ |, and 

- 464 the stress regime is compressional (see hatched regions in Figure 5(a-b)). The shallow 

- 465 extent of these zones is principally a reflection of the low lithostatic vertical stress at 

- 466 shallow depths, which is exceeded by only a modest horizontal compression. 

- 467 

- 468 The presence of a slipping fault perturbs the differential stress in its vicinity, imposing a 

- 469 failure shadow on a crust that is otherwise at frictional yield. This failure shadow 

- 470 broadens near the surface, particularly within the footwall block, and tapers to a narrow 

- 471 width near the BDT. Figure 5(c) shows that frictional-plastic strain does not accumulate 

- 472 within the shadow, but rather the greatest accumulation is outside the shadow in the 

- 473 hanging wall block. In this region, we observe frictional plastic shear-bands dipping at 

- 474 45°, synthetic to the main fault, and intersecting the surface ( _z_ =0) outside the edge of the 

- 475 failure shadow. Low levels of frictional plasticity are also observed in the footwall block 

- 476 at depths of 3-7 km where upward-directed fault slip and compression have not 

- 477 significantly altered the stress state. Except for a small region at the root of the fault, the 

- 478 majority of the fault plane is free from plastic deformation, although we cannot preclude 

- 479 failure from dynamic loading during rupture propagation, which we have not modeled. 

- 480 

- 481 Previous numerical experiments by Melosh and Williams (1989) indicated that formation 

- 482 of antithetic faults in the hanging wall of a main fault was energetically favorable over 

- 483 synthetic faults of similar a similar dip. This result was derived assuming immediate 

- 484 secondary fault formation in response to failure stresses induced by slip on the main fault, 

- 485 i.e., coseismic antithetic faults. A similar result is replicated in our model by considering 

- 486 frictional-plastic strain accumulated during the coseismic period in response to slip on a 

- 487 65° dipping master fault (Figure 6(b)). For the model containing a 50° dipping fault, 

- 488 frictional-plastic strain during the seismic period is negligible as local bending at the 

- 489 surface of the hanging wall is less pronounced that for steeper faults. This result suggests 

- 490 a preference for coseismic antithetic faulting may be limited to steeper dip angles. 

- 491 

- 492 In contrast, in Figure 5(c) and Figure 6(b) we demonstrate a preference for synthetic fault 

- 493 formation, caused by a steady accumulation of frictional-plastic strain in shear bands 

- 494 during the comparatively longer interseismic period. It should be emphasized this result 

- 495 is not inconsistent with Melosh and Williams (1989), but rather indicates that the 

- 496 configuration of secondary faults is sensitive to the fault dip relative to optimally oriented 

- 497 fault dip, and to the stage of the seismic cycle during which they form.

- 499 **7. Fault plane rotation** 

- 500 Coseismic displacement of the fault blocks parallel to the fault plane, which 

- 501 accommodates extension and reduces near-fault shear stresses, is perhaps the most 

- 502 conspicuous aspect of deformation associated with faulting (e.g., Barrientos et al., 1987; 

- 503 Segal, 2010). However, observations of normal faults dipping at shallow angles (<30°) 

- 504 that preclude reactivation within an Andersonian stress regime (Wernicke, 1995), or a 

- 505 regime of cross-cutting faults (Profett, 1977; Nur et al., 1986), suggest a secondary 

- 506 process of rotation via a bookshelf or domino-style mechanism. Such motion, resolved on 

- 507 the fault plane, is equivalent to a displacement of the fault block perpendicular to the fault 

- 508 plane. Further, for a rotation as opposed to a rigid body translation, displacement must 

- 509 increase linearly with distance from some pivot, i.e., a point of no displacement. 

- 510 

- 511 Figure 7 plots fault perpendicular displacement along the fault and against depth during 

- 512 (a) the interseismic and (b) seismic periods, with a regression line fit to the data in the 

- 513 brittle layer (<8 km depth). During both periods, displacement profiles in the upper crust 

- 514 are approximately linear and have gradients of 59 and 66 mm km<sup>-1</sup> (i.e., millimeters of 

- 515 fault perpendicular displacement per kilometer of depth) for the interseismic and seismic 

- 516 periods, respectively. 

- 517 

- 518 The magnitude of fault plane rotation can be calculated from the gradients of the 

- 519 regression lines in Figure 7. A linear fault-normal displacement profile, _u_ , is expressed as 

- 520 a function of depth, _u_ = _m_ ( _z-z0_ ), where _m_ is the gradient of regression line through the 

- 521 displacement data, and _z_ 0 is the depth of the fixed horizontal axis, or pivot, about which 

- 522 rotation occurs. The depth of the pivot corresponds to the intersection of the regression 

- 523 line in Figure 7(a-b) with the vertical _u =_ 0 axis. During the interseismic period, the pivot 

- 524 ranges in depth from 18 to 18.7 km, with a mean of 18.4 km. For the seismic period, 

- 525 rotation of the fault plane occurs about a pivot at a depth of 4.7 km. A displacement, _u_ , 

- 526 some vertical distance _z_ - _z_ 0 above the pivot induces a rotation, _d_ θ, of the fault plane 

- 527 (Figure 7(c)), given by 

- 528 _d_ = tan− 1 ( _u_ sin/( _z_ − _z_ 0 )) = tan− 1 ( _m_ .sin) (6) 

- 529 Using m = 59 and 66 mm km<sup>-1</sup> , we calculate a shallowing of dip of 2.4 and 2.7 

- 530 millidegrees during the interseismic and seismic periods, respectively. This corresponds 

- 531 to a long term rotation rate of 23.5° every million years (for a seismic cycle that repeats 

- 532 every 220 years), although a reduction in this rate would presumably be observed as the 

- 533 fault rotates out of the range for reactivation. 

- 534 

- 535 The total rotation during a seismic cycle is distributed approximately equally between the 

- 536 interseismic and seismic periods. The amount of coseismic rotation is proportional to the 

- 537 length of the interseismic period that precedes it, and is thus also proportional to 

- 538 earthquake magnitude. Further, coseismic rotation about an axis at a depth of 4.7 km, 14 

- 539 km shallower than that for the interseismic period, suggests that a different forcing 

- 540 operates each rotation. For instance, during the interseismic period, far-field tectonic 

- 541 extension deforms the entire crust, causing vertical contraction in addition to horizontal 

- 542 spreading, effectively flattening the crust and the fault that is hosted within. The effect of 

- 543 this deformation on fault dip is evident in Figure 8(a), which presents the exaggerated 

- 544 change in position of the fault surface and topography during the interseismic period. 

- 545 

- 546 In contrast, displacements at the lateral boundaries during the short seismic period are too 

- 547 small to account for the observed rotation. However, large and rapid stress changes in the 

- 548 mid-crust, which cause uplift of the footwall and accommodate collapse of the hanging 

- 549 wall, may be responsible. Footwall uplift is caused by a decrease in stress and rapid 

- 550 decompression of the crust between 6 and 9 km depth of the footwall block. Figure 8(b) 

- 551 shows the decrease in pressure associated with this uplift, superimposed on the deformed 

- 552 topography and fault plane. A drop in pressure corresponds to an increase in volume in 

- 553 that region, which, while primarily directed up-dip along the fault plane, also includes a 

- 554 fault normal component. This expansion applies a force to the hanging wall and a torque 

- 555 about the pivot at 4.7 km depth. This expansion also accounts for a component of the 

- 556 pressure increase in the adjacent hanging wall. The expanding footwall does work on the 

- 557 hanging wall, which, in addition to the compression caused by dip-slip of the fault block 

- 558 above, yields a reduction in volume and an increase in stress. As this rotation occurs due 

- 559 to stress changes at the fault tip, the depth of the pivot about which rotation occurs is 

- 560 primarily controlled by the depth of faulting.

- 562 **8. Evolution of normal fault systems** 

- 563 Where pre-existing faults do not adequately accommodate the geodetic extension rate, 

- 564 parts of the crust are subjected to a horizontal, positive strain. In regions of the crust 

- 565 where the shear stress is below the Mohr-Coulomb failure limit, extension is 

- 566 accommodated by elastic strain. However, continued elastic stretching generates shear 

- 567 stresses that eventually induce failure on any pre-existing fracture planes and material 

- 568 discontinuities. Shear stresses are maintained at near critical values (Townend and 

- 569 Zoback, 2000) by incremental frictional failure on these minor structures in response to 

- 570 each subsequent increment of extension. These processes constrain the maximum stress 

- 571 state attainable by the crust and therefore impose an upper limit on the amount of 

- 572 extension that is temporarily accommodated as elastic strain. 

- 573 

- 574 However, no such constraint exists for accommodation of extension through frictional- 

- 575 plastic strain, which accumulates following the saturation of elastic strain. In our model, 

- 576 frictional plastic deformation localizes in shear bands that form at 45° to the maximum 

- 577 principal compressional stress, which is vertical in an extensional tectonic regime. That 

- 578 these shear bands represent the development of new faults is speculative, although they 

- 579 do satisfy two criteria associated with fault structures: (i) the shear bands accommodate 

- 580 dip-slip displacement and (ii) are localized, rather than distributed (Schueller et al., 

- 581 2010), zones of deformation, in this case the width of several elements (approximately 1 

- 582 km). In these models, shear zone width is sensitive to mesh dimension, as we have not 

- 583 incorporated a description of strain softening behavior (Needleman, 1988). Physically, a 

- 584 localization of frictional plasticity represents a continuous reactivation within a particular 

- 585 region of those small discontinuities, e.g., joints, dilational jogs, fractures, etc., 

- 586 accommodating shear displacements. Providing each subsequent reactivation increases 

- 587 the length of the activated structure, a region of frictional plastic shear may be regarded 

- 588 as a sort of proto-fault. We note that the evolution of a proto-fault-style shear zone into a 

- 589 true fault that perturbs stresses in its vicinity is not modeled here. Instead, we are 

- 590 considering the early onset of proto-fault development. 

- 591 

- 592 Basinward migration of normal fault structures has been inferred from stratigraphic 

- 593 studies in Turkey (Dart et al., 1995), the central Basin and Range province, USA (Horton 

- 594 and Schmitt, 1998), the Gulf of Corinth, Greece (Goldsworthy and Jackson, 2001), and 

- 595 the strike-slip Gar Basin in western Tibet (Sanchez et al., 2010). Structurally, new faults 

- 596 develop in the hanging wall block of older faults producing a series of sub-parallel faults 

- 597 spaced every 2-5 km. Goldsworthy and Jackson (2001) proposed several mechanisms for 

- 598 the cessation of rupture on one fault and concurrent development of an adjacent structure 

- 599 including: topographic stresses in a domino-style topography modulated by the strength 

- 600 of the active fault; density contrast between sedimentation of the hanging wall basin and 

- 601 erosion of the uplifted footwall; and rolling-hinge evolution along a migrating lower 

- 602 crustal flow front (Buck, 1988; McKenzie et al., 2000). However, they note that, with the 

- 603 exception of the rolling hinge, which itself requires a migrating magma source, these 

- 604 mechanisms do not account for the preferred basinward direction of migration. 

- 605 Alternatively, inward migration within a rift may result from brittle necking of strong 

- 606 crustal layers, where thinner brittle layers are easier to fault cf. thicker brittle layers 

- 607 outside the rift (Buiter et al., 2006). 

- 608 

- 609 The migration of fault activity first requires that new faults form, which occurs when 

- 610 existing faults do not accommodate the total geodetic extension rate. For instance, in 

- 611 Figure 3 we show that for a 220 year interseismic period, a single fault dipping at 50° 

- 612 accounts for approximately half the extension applied to the upper crust, with the balance 

- 613 accommodated by off-fault frictional-plastic strain. As fault slip and frictional-plastic 

- 614 strain accumulate during the seismic and interseismic periods respectively, we consider 

- 615 the competing accumulation of elastic versus frictional-plastic strain during the 

- 616 interseismic period, with the caveat that the elastic component is replaced by fault slip 

- 617 during an earthquake. Elastic strain accumulates only in those regions of the upper crust 

- 618 maintained below the limit of frictional plastic failure, which is largely within the near- 

- 619 fault stress-failure shadow (Figure 5(a)). Elsewhere, tectonic extension applied for long 

- 620 time periods ensures the crust is close to failure and thus strain is accommodated by 

- 621 frictional plasticity. 

- 622 

- 623 For a system containing a single fault, frictional-plastic strain accommodates a significant 

- 624 component of the extension budget (Figure 4(b)). In terms of rift basin evolution, our 

- 625 model describes an area soon after the onset of extension in which only a single mature 

- 626 fault developed (see Figure 9(a)). The full extension rate cannot be accommodated by this 

- 627 structure, and thus frictional-plastic strain accommodates the shortfall. Frictional-plastic 

- 628 strain localizes into shear zones in the hanging wall. In our model these shear zones are 

- 629 synthetic to the main fault, and are therefore consistent with a scenario of migration of 

- 630 fault activity into the hanging walls of existing faults. However, the formation of 

- 631 secondary faults antithetic to the main fault, i.e., a graben geometry, is observed in many 

- 632 locations (e.g., Jackson and McKenzie, 1983) and also in numerical experiments (Melosh 

- 633 and Williams, 1989). Preference of one over the other has been considered from an 

- 634 energy minimization perspective (Melosh and Williams, 1989); however, Figures 5 and 6 

- 635 indicate that the geometry of the primary fault, and the precise phase of the seismic cycle 

- 636 during which secondary fault formation occurs, may also play a role. Other factors that 

- 637 may control the geometry of secondary structures include the initial localization of shear 

- 638 bands onto pre-existing planes of weakness or crustal heterogeneities, and the effects of 

- 639 gravitational spreading and basal detachment shear during rifting (e.g., Brun et al., 1994). 

- 640 

- 641 Localization of frictional plastic deformation into proto-faults occurs outside the stress- 

- 642 failure shadow of the main fault in the hanging wall block (Figure 9(b)). The extent of the 

- 643 failure shadow therefore exercise a degree of control on the location of these proto-faults 

- 644 and perhaps also the spacing of a sub-parallel array of faults. If the -1 MPa contour is 

- 645 used to estimate the dimension of the failure shadow, Figure 5(a) would suggest a 

- 646 minimum spacing of 10 km for footwall faults and 2 km for hanging wall faults. The 

- 647 latter is slightly smaller than the average fault spacing in the western Gulf of Corinth of 

- 648 5-8 km (Rohais et al., 2007); however, as the dimensions of the stress-failure shadow 

- 649 depend on the thickness of the brittle upper crust and fault dip, direct comparison 

- 650 between model and field studies is not possible. For example, crustal thickness in the 

- 651 Gulf of Corinth of 10-12 km (Briole et al., 2000) is somewhat thicker than the 6-8 km in 

- 652 our model. For this crust, we would expect larger failure shadows than those modeled and 

- 653 consequently larger minimum fault spacings for both fault blocks. In addition, fault dips 

- 654 in the western Gulf of Corinth are not well constrained, although the dip of our modeled 

- 655 fault planes are consistent with those used by Briole et al. (2000) in modeling normal 

- 656 fault earthquakes in the Corinth Rift. 

- 657 

- 658 Coalescence of frictional plastic deformation into a single shear zone, culminating in a 

- 659 new discrete fault structure has important implications for the extension budget. The 

- 660 introduction of a second stress-failure shadow (Figure 9(c)) increases the area available 

- 661 for interseismic elastic-strain accumulation and reduces that available for frictional- 

- 662 plastic straining. Proto-faults previously developing adjacent to the new fault would 

- 663 become inactive as deformation within its stress-failure shadow is switched to 

- 664 elastic/fault slip (Figure 9). Fault slip will then account for a greater component of the 

- 665 extension budget. Frictional-plastic straining will initiate in the hanging wall of the new 

- 666 fault, although at a reduced rate reflecting the extension accommodated by the second 

- 667 fault. 

- 668 

- 669 Rotation of faults to shallower dip angles, either through interseismic stretching or 

- 670 coseismic stress changes in the mid-crust (see section 7), has the opposite effect on the 

- 671 upper crustal extension budget. The eventual removal of a fault that was accommodating 

- 672 extension (Figure 9(d)), and the subsequent recovery of stresses within its failure shadow 

- 673 to Mohr Coulomb yield (Figure 9(e)), increases the extension shortfall, which is then 

- 674 accommodated by frictional plasticity. The formation of proto-faults in the hanging wall 

- 675 of the second fault then accelerates and the cycle continues. Alternatively, the recovery of 

- 676 the stress-failure shadow of the inactive fault would permit the development of new, 

- 677 cross-cutting faults in this region (Nur et al., 1986). 

- 678 

- 679 The net effect of the processes discussed above is a preference for the migration of 

- 680 faulting into the hanging walls of existing faults. However, an appropriate timescale to 

- 681 attach to this evolutionary scenario is unclear, largely due to ambiguity in the rate of 

- 682 formation of new faults. In our models, the frictional plastic shear strain rate in the 

- 683 hanging wall where proto-faults form is approximately 7×10<sup>-16</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> , which for faulting to 

- 684 seismogenic depths (6-8 km) represents a slip rate on the order of 0.15 mm yr<sup>-1</sup> . At this 

- 685 rate, a proto-fault should exhibit several 10's of meters of offset after several hundred kyr. 

- 686 Goldsworthy and Jackson (2001) described uplift of coastal faults in the western Gulf of 

- 687 Corinth and suggested that the morphology might be generated in less than a million 

- 688 years. One might further expect acceleration of the slip rate with increasing rift maturity 

- 689 due to along-strike linkage of fault segments (Cowie and Roberts, 2001; Bell et al., 

- 690 2009).

- 692 **9. Conclusion** 

- 693 The onset of extension and subsequent evolution of normal faulting within the brittle 

- 694 crust was investigated via a computational model for an extending elastic-viscous-plastic 

- 695 crust. Extension applied at the boundaries of the model is accommodated in the upper 

- 696 crust through dip-slip on a fault contact surface or the accumulation of frictional-plastic 

- 697 strain, and in the lower crust through viscous deformation. Between these two layers, a 

- 698 mixed brittle-viscous region develops, which exhibits a rapid decrease in frictional- 

- 699 plastic straining, a slower decay in fault slip, and the onset of viscous deformation. 

- 700 

- 701 Frictional-plastic strain localizes preferentially within the hanging wall due to the 

- 702 distribution of shear stress near the fault. Coseismic displacements of the fault blocks 

- 703 create a stress-failure shadow that extends disproportionally into the footwall thereby 

- 704 favoring the formation of new faults in the adjacent hanging wall block. The dimension 

- 705 of the failure shadow at the surface is broadly consistent with the observed fault spacing 

- 706 in normal fault arrays of several kms. 

- 707 

- 708 Rotation of the fault plane about a horizontal axis at depth is observed to occur during 

- 709 both phases of the seismic cycle. A long term rotation rate of 23.5<sup>o</sup> per Myr is divided 

- 710 into almost equal components of far-field tectonic rotation during the interseismic period 

- 711 and rapid rotation induced by mid-crustal stress changes during the seismic period. The 

- 712 subsequent shallowing of the fault plane over many seismic cycles provides one avenue 

- 713 by which faults may be become inactive, accelerating the frictional-plastic straining and 

- 714 proto-fault formation accommodates the extension shortfall. 

- 715 

- 716 The juxtaposition of these competing processes within an extending crust is consistent 

- 717 with evolution of subsequent faults into existing hanging walls. However, a basinward 

- 718 migration of faulting cannot occur indefinitely without a corresponding migration of the 

- 719 locus of extension. Where the position of extension is static in time, recovery of the 

- 720 stress-failure shadow around inactive faults may permit the eventual return of faulting to 

- 721 the older footwalls. Cross-cutting of old, shallow structures by younger, steeper faults is 

- 722 permitted, providing that they form outside the stress-failure shadow that extends 

- 723 disproportionally into the footwall block. 

- 724 

- 725 As a final note, this simplified model for normal fault evolution requires careful 

- 726 interpretation in the context of a richly complex crust. Material properties, stratigraphy 

- 727 and tectonic forcings that vary along-strike and with depth also exert important controls 

- 728 on the evolution of deformation and stress around faults, and by extension the 

- 729 development of new structures. The mechanisms and scenarios described here do not 

- 730 constitute a complete or prescriptive theory, but rather serve as an illustration of the 

- 731 evolutionary processes that may prevail in specific locales, e.g., the western Gulf of 

- 732 Corinth.
