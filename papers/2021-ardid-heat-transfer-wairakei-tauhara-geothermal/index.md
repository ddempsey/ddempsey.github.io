## In brief

We combined electrical surveys, clay tests on drill cuttings, and well temperature logs to map the clay layer above the Wairakei-Tauhara geothermal reservoir. We estimate that 380 ± 21 MW of heat passes up through this layer. We treat this as a lower bound on the field's total heat output.

- We mapped the clay cap at Wairakei-Tauhara from 250 magnetotelluric stations, with its depth constrained by clay tests on cuttings from 50 wells.
- Temperatures at the top and bottom of the clay cap averaged 58°C and 199°C, with 95% ranges of about 20 to 120°C and 130 to 250°C.
- A deep electrical conductor at Aratiatia Flats lies in cold rock. We suggest it formed by low-temperature alteration of ignimbrite.
- Conduction alone accounts for 153 ± 18 MW through the clay cap. Including slow upward water flow raises our estimate to 380 ± 21 MW.
- We treat 380 MW as a lower bound on the field's heat output, because it omits heat that leaves the reservoir sideways beneath the cap.

## The problem

A geothermal well sited on a misinterpreted survey can encounter only dry,
cool rock. Such a well costs about 10 million US dollars. Because geothermal
reservoirs are buried, finding and characterising them relies on surveys made
from above (Cumming, 2009). A reservoir is usually overlain by rock of low
permeability, often clay formed where hot water has altered the rock. This
clay cap separates hot reservoir fluid from cold groundwater near the surface.
Its shape can constrain the models used to explore and manage a field.

Clay of this kind conducts electricity well. Magnetotelluric (MT) surveys are
now the preferred way to map the resulting electrical structure (Johnston et
al., 1992). However, conductive clays also form outside active hydrothermal
systems. In the Taupō Volcanic Zone, ignimbrites held at low temperatures for
a long time have altered to conductive clays and zeolites. This has led to
misinterpretation of resistivity surveys (Bibby et al., 2005).

The Wairakei-Tauhara field lies on the northeast shore of Lake Taupō. Its heat
output was previously estimated at about 530 MW from its chloride flux, the
rate at which dissolved chloride flows out of the field (Bibby et al., 1995;
Fisher, 1964). This method has large uncertainties. Our goal in this study was
to map the clay cap across the field, test what temperatures it formed at, and
estimate how much heat passes through it.

## What we did

We used data from a 2010 MT survey of 250 stations (Sepulveda et al., 2012).
At each station, a Bayesian inversion (Ardid et al., 2021) produced many
three-layer models consistent with the data, with the middle layer limited to
a resistivity of 1 to 5 ohm metres, typical of conductive clays. We also used
methylene blue (MeB) tests on drill cuttings from 50 wells, which measure clay
content (Gunderson et al., 2000). These gave starting ranges, or priors, for
the depths of the top and bottom of the conductor. This is because MT data
alone resolve the lower boundary poorly.

Next, we read temperatures at the top and bottom of the cap from logs in 122
wells. Finally, we fitted a one-dimensional model of heat conduction plus
upward water flow (Bredehoeft and Papadopulos, 1965) to each well's
temperatures within the cap.

## What we found

In the centre of the field, the clay cap largely follows the Huka Falls
Formation, a sequence of low-permeability lake sediments. The cap averages
about 316 m thick. Across the 4 km of the main upflow, its top and bottom
roughly follow the 100°C and 180 to 200°C isotherms. However, outside the
upflow the 180°C isotherm dips more steeply than the clay.

Temperatures at the top and bottom of the cap averaged 58°C and 199°C. This is
consistent with the 50 to 200°C range over which smectite clays form (Browne,
1978). However, the ranges containing 95% of the values were about 20 to 120°C
at the top and 130 to 250°C at the bottom. We therefore caution against
inferring temperature from clay alone, particularly where no well confirms the
clay.

At Aratiatia Flats, MT shows a conductor deeper than 400 m outside the usual
field boundary. Wells there are cold, and the conductor lies in the Wairakei
ignimbrite. We suggest it formed by diagenesis, slow alteration at low
temperature.

Using a typical thermal conductivity of 2.2 W/m/°C, the median temperature
gradient through the cap of 476°C/km corresponds to a conductive heat flux of
about 1.05 W/m². Summed over the field, conduction accounts for 153 ± 18 MW.

In the model, faster upflow of hot water steepens the temperature gradient
near the top of the cap and flattens it below. In a calculation of our own,
not reported in the paper, a value of about 1.6 for β, a number comparing heat
carried by rising water with heat conducted through rock, doubles the heat
flux at the top and raises the temperature halfway down the cap from 129°C to
155°C. Fitted to the wells, conduction plus upflow gives a median heat flux of
2.15 W/m², or a total of 380 ± 21 MW when summed over the field. The fits
imply water rising through the cap at roughly 53 mm a year, with a 95% range
of about 4 to 230 mm.

We interpret 380 MW as a lower bound on the heat output of the system. This is
because it omits heat that leaves the reservoir sideways beneath the cap, or
through narrow permeable paths that wells have not sampled (Hunt et al.,
2009). For scale, 380 MW is the output of about 190,000 two-kilowatt heaters
running continuously. The earlier estimate of 530 MW is about 40% higher than
ours. The difference may arise because our estimate is a lower bound and
because chloride-based estimates have uncertainties of about 25% (Bibby et
al., 1995).

## Limitations

Our model assumes that temperatures in the cap are still those of the natural
state before development began in the 1950s. About 60 years of production and
reinjection have probably disturbed temperatures, particularly in the
reservoir below. By conduction alone, such a disturbance would reach about 43
m into the cap, or 14% of its average thickness. We therefore expect its
effect on our heat fluxes to be second-order at most.

The one-dimensional model gives only an average flow rate for each well, and
the conductive estimate ignores variations in rock properties. In early
exploration, before MT and temperature data exist, chloride-based methods may
still be preferred.

## Glossary

A **geothermal reservoir** is a body of permeable rock holding hot water that
can be produced through wells.

The **clay cap** is a layer of low-permeability clay, formed by hot water
altering rock, that overlies a geothermal reservoir.

**Permeability** is a measure of how easily water flows through rock.

**Magnetotellurics (MT)** is a surface electromagnetic survey that estimates
how electrical resistivity varies with depth.

**Methylene blue (MeB)** is a dye whose uptake by drill cuttings measures
their content of conductive clay.

A **Bayesian inversion** returns a range of models consistent with both the
data and prior information.

**Smectite** is a clay mineral, formed by hot water at about 50 to 200°C, that
conducts electricity well.

An **ignimbrite** is a rock formed from the deposits of an explosive volcanic
eruption.

**Diagenesis** is the slow alteration of rock at low temperature after it is
deposited.

An **isotherm** is a line joining points of equal temperature.

**Resistivity** is how strongly a material resists an electric current,
measured in ohm metres. A low resistivity marks a good electrical conductor.

The **upflow** is the zone where hot water rises from the reservoir towards
the surface.

**Heat flux** is the rate of heat flow through a unit area, in watts per
square metre (W/m²). Summed over an area it gives heat flow in megawatts (MW).

**Zeolites** are minerals formed when volcanic rock is altered by water at low
temperature.

A **prior** is the range of values a parameter is allowed to take before the
data are considered.

## Abstract

To obtain the fullest picture of geothermal systems, it is necessary to integrate different types of data, for example, surface electromagnetic surveys, lithology, geochemistry, and temperature logs. Here, by joint modeling a multichannel data set we quantify the spatial distribution of heat transfer through the hydrothermally altered, impermeable smectite layer that has developed atop the Wairakei‐Tauhara system, New Zealand. Our approach involves first constraining magnetotelluric inversion models with methylene blue analysis (an indicator of conductive clay) and mapping these onto temperature and lithology data from geothermal wells. Then, one‐dimensional models are fitted to the temperature data to estimate heat flux variations across the field. As a result, we have been able to map the primary seal that insulates the geothermal reservoir and estimate the heat flow of the system. The approach could be applied in geothermal provinces around the world with implications for sustainable resource management and our understanding of these magmatic systems.
