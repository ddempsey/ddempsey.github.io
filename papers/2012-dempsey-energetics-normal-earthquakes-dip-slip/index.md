## In brief

We modelled a normal fault in a stretching crust and quantified where energy is released and taken up during each earthquake. Stored elastic energy lifts the footwall. The hangingwall drops under gravity and compresses the crust. We suggest the energy for normal faulting comes from gravity and stored elastic energy.

- Between earthquakes, the total elastic strain energy in our model crust decreases, although it increases locally in the footwall at mid-crustal depths.
- During an earthquake, the footwall releases about 0.85 TJ of strain energy. Of this, 0.43 TJ lifts the footwall, 0.14 TJ heats the fault and 0.28 TJ pushes the hangingwall.
- The falling hangingwall releases about 1.0 TJ of gravitational potential energy. This is the main source of work that compresses the crust and adds about 1.3 TJ of strain energy.
- Elastic strain energy increases through much of the upper crust during an earthquake. As the fault blocks move apart, each pushes horizontally against the crust beyond it.
- We suggest tectonic stretching triggers normal earthquakes by shifting the balance of forces on the fault, and the energy they release comes from gravity and stored elastic strain.

## The problem

Earthquakes release large amounts of energy, but often only a small fraction
of it (the seismic efficiency) is radiated as the seismic waves felt as
shaking (McGarr, 1994; Kanamori, 2001). The rest moves, deforms and heats the
crust.

The standard description of an earthquake is elastic rebound (Reid, 1911).
Tectonic forces slowly strain the rock either side of a locked fault. The
strained rock stores elastic strain energy. When the fault slips, that energy
is released as the rock returns towards its unstrained shape. Reid developed
this model for strike-slip faults, where the two sides move horizontally and
gravity does no work.

Normal faults form where the crust is being stretched. In a normal earthquake
the block above the fault, the hangingwall, drops. Geodetic surveys, which
repeatedly measure ground elevation, record this fall (Barrientos et al.,
1987). Gravitational potential energy released by the fall contributes to the
energy budget of these earthquakes (McKenzie, 1978; Stewart, 1980). Our goal
was to quantify that budget for a normal fault, and to find where in the crust
energy is given up and taken up during the earthquake cycle.

## What we did

Following Ellis et al. (2006), we built a two-dimensional finite element model
of a crust 60 km wide and 25 km deep. Its sides were pulled apart at a
combined 12 mm per year, or about 1.2 m per century. The model rock deforms
elastically, fails by frictional sliding on small fractures near the surface,
and creeps slowly at depth. The transition from brittle to ductile behaviour
occurs where temperatures exceed about 350 °C.

The crust contains one fault, which dips at 50° from horizontal to a depth of
15 km. We locked the fault for 250 years with infinite friction. We then
lowered its friction coefficient (the ratio of the force needed to slide the
fault to the force pressing it shut) to 0.2 for 60 seconds to represent an
earthquake. The model is static and does not simulate seismic waves. We report
energies from the 12th cycle, once the stress pattern repeated from one cycle
to the next.

Each model earthquake produced 1.2 m of vertical offset at the surface, as the
footwall rose and the hangingwall dropped. This pattern is similar in shape to
measurements after the 1983 Borah Peak (Idaho, magnitude 6.9) and 1959 Hebgen
Lake (Montana, magnitude 7.3) earthquakes (Barrientos et al., 1987). Our
offset corresponds to a smaller earthquake, about magnitude 5.8.

We balanced the work done by gravity and by the model boundaries against the
change in elastic strain energy and the energy lost to creep, to sliding on
small fractures, and to friction on the fault (Cooke and Murphy, 2004). We
split the elastic strain energy into a volumetric part, from changes in rock
volume, and a distortional part, from changes in rock shape.

## What we found

In our model, the total elastic strain energy of the crust decreases between
earthquakes, although it increases locally in the footwall at 8 to 10 km
depth. This is because the weight of overlying rock keeps the crust in
compression almost everywhere. Stretching reduces that compression and lowers
the stored energy. The crust therefore does work, expending stored energy, as
it extends.

During an earthquake, the footwall rebounds elastically. A drop in stress and
an expansion of rock at 8 to 10 km depth release about 0.85 TJ of elastic
strain energy. Of this, 0.43 TJ lifts the footwall, 0.14 TJ heats the fault by
friction, and 0.28 TJ pushes on the hangingwall.

The hangingwall is driven by gravity. Next to the fault it subsides by up to 1
m at the surface. This fall releases about 1.0 TJ of gravitational potential
energy. Together with 0.1 TJ of distortional strain energy and the 0.28 TJ
from the footwall, this energy compresses the crust. It creates about 1.3 TJ
of volumetric strain energy, particularly at 6 to 10 km depth where slip on
the fault stops, and heats the fault by another 0.14 TJ.

As the two fault blocks move apart, each pushes horizontally against the crust
beyond it. This compresses most of the upper crust. The increase in volumetric
strain energy there is about 50% larger than the simultaneous release of
distortional strain energy. The net elastic strain energy of the crust
therefore rises during an earthquake.

We use a spring-and-block model to explain why the hangingwall slips. Gravity
pulls the block down the fault, and horizontal compression pushes it up the
fault. Stretching reduces the horizontal push and leaves the gravitational
pull unchanged. When the net down-dip force, which pulls the block down the
fault, exceeds friction, the block slides down, recompresses the crust and
restores the balance. Gravitational potential energy is present throughout the
earthquake cycle but does work only when the fault slips. We suggest tectonic
stretching shifts the balance of forces on a crust that is already loaded, and
the energy of normal faulting comes from gravity and stored elastic strain.

## Limitations

The model is a single two-dimensional cross-section through one fault. The
model earthquake is smaller than the Borah Peak and Hebgen Lake events. The
difference may reflect greater stress build-up on those faults or different
rock stiffness (Gudmundsson, 2004). The model also neglects seismic waves and
the way fault friction changes with slip speed (rate-dependent friction).
Estimating radiated energy and slip after an earthquake would require a model
that includes them.

## Glossary

A **normal fault** is a fault where the crust is being stretched and the upper
block slides down the fault plane.

The **hangingwall** is the block of crust above a dipping fault, and the
**footwall** is the block below it.

**Elastic strain energy** is energy stored in rock that is compressed or
distorted and can be recovered when the rock returns to its original shape.

**Volumetric** strain energy comes from changes in rock volume, and
**distortional** strain energy comes from changes in rock shape.

**Gravitational potential energy** is energy a mass has because of its height.
It is released when the mass moves down.

**Elastic rebound** is the release of stored strain energy when a fault slips
and the surrounding rock relaxes.

The **brittle–ductile transition** is the depth below which rock deforms
mainly by slow creep.

**Seismic efficiency** is the fraction of an earthquake's energy that is
radiated as seismic waves.

A **geodetic survey** repeatedly measures the position and elevation of points
on the ground.

A **friction coefficient** is the ratio of the force needed to slide a fault
to the force pressing it shut.

A fault **dips** at the angle it is inclined below horizontal. **Down-dip**
means down the slope of the fault.

**Rate-dependent friction** is fault friction that changes with the speed of
slip.

A **finite element model** solves the equations of deformation by dividing the
crust into many small elements.

A **terajoule (TJ)** is one million million joules.

The elastic rebound model for earthquakes recognizes that elastic strain energy does work displacing, deforming, and accelerating the crust, as well as causing frictional heating. In this paper we present an energy budget for dip-slip fault rupture in an extensional tectonic regime. A computational model of an elastic-plastic-viscous crust hosting a single fault, modeled as two surfaces in frictional contact, demonstrates contrasting energy flows between the hanging-wall and footwall fault blocks.
