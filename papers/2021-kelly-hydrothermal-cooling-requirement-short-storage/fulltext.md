**Abstract** Large (>50 km<sup>3</sup> ) magma bodies that contribute to caldera-forming eruptions in the Taupō Volcanic Zone (TVZ), New Zealand have shown evidence for crystallization and storage on the order of decades to centuries prior to eruption. We compare various conductive and convective heat extraction models to determine which is more likely to enable the heat loss required for short magma storage. We conclude that purely conductive models only account for heat loss on millennial or longer timescales. We also show that convective hydrothermal systems with heat output of 1,000 MW in magnitude are required for decadal heat extraction from a large, contiguous magma body; yet, heat output similar to present-day conditions would be suitable for cooling magma distributed as a patchwork of smaller magma bodies. This study shows the potential connection with heat flow at the surface and the presence of a magma body, providing future directions for monitoring restless calderas in environments with a wealth of hydrothermal activity like the TVZ.

**Plain Language Summary** Previous research has shown that eruptible magma bodies in the Taupō Volcanic Zone (TVZ), New Zealand may only have been stored in the crust for less than 100 years. These magma bodies have contributed to highly explosive, caldera-forming eruptions, so understanding the timescales over which heat is lost from these magma bodies prior to eruption could be beneficial for monitoring these caldera environments. In order to understand how these magma bodies can lose heat this quickly, we model heat loss through conductive and convective methods, taking advantage of the existence of hydrothermal systems that currently reside in the TVZ. We find that hydrothermal systems are required for rapid heat loss over decades to centuries. Knowing the scale of the power output from such a hydrothermal system could be important for understanding how quickly possible eruptible magma can be cooled, and could be combined with measurements determining the presence or absence of magma to assess potential hazards in the TVZ and beyond.

## **1. Introduction** 

Ignimbrite-producing, caldera-forming eruptions are some of the most intense, explosive eruptions on the planet, occurring in a wide variety of tectonic environments (Bryan et al., 2010; de Silva & Gosnold, 2007; Elston, 1984; Gravley et al., 2016; Lipman, 1984). Understanding the heat flux associated with large caldera-forming eruptions is key since, if there is a significant increase in heat flux of a hydrothermal system that is already in existence when a large silicic magma body is being stored, then the measurement of heat flux at the surface of calderas in a state of unrest may offer insight into the eruptibility of magma below the surface. 

Crystal growth times inferred from geochronometry and geochronology suggest that magma bodies are typically not stored for longer than centuries to a few millennia (Allan et al., 2013; Charlier et al., 2008; K. M. Cooper & Kent, 2014; Druitt et al., 2012; Gualda & Sutton, 2016; Gualda et al., 2018; Gualda, Pamukcu, et al., 2012; Pamukcu, Gualda, Bégué, & Gravley, 2015; Shamloo & Till, 2019). In the Taupō Volcanic Zone (TVZ), New Zealand, crystallization may be even shorter, spanning only decades to a few centuries, as demonstrated by Ti-in-quartz geochronometry (Gualda et al., 2018). This suggests that eruptible magma bodies in this region are only stored for decades to centuries, at relatively constant pressures, prior to catastrophic, caldera-forming eruptions (Gualda et al., 2018; Pamukcu, Gualda, Bégué, & Gravley, 2015). While Ti diffusivities in quartz have been recently questioned (Jollands et al., 2020a, 2020b; see also Audétat

Here we emphasize that crystallization timescales constrain the magma cooling timescales in the crust, as well as storage timescales of an eruptible magma. 

The question then arises: how is heat extracted so that crystallizing, eruptible magma is only stored for decades to centuries? In order for a magma body to crystallize, it must lose heat, which includes sensible heat loss, which leads to decreasing temperatures, and latent heat loss, which is necessary for crystallization of different mineral phases and bubble exsolution (Perrot, 1998). The complexity of the problem increases if convection within the magma chamber is taken into account; however, a convecting rhyolite can accelerate the cooling of a reservoir by 15% (Andersen & Weis, 2020). While we do not consider the effect of convection in the magma body, any effect would only decrease heat extraction timescales calculated in this study. Heat extraction from magma bodies as a result of conductive heat flow has been previously studied (Annen et al., 2015; Gualda, Pamukcu, et al., 2012), as well as the impact of permeability of surrounding rock on the mode of heat flow (Hayba & Ingebritsen, 1997), the longevity of hydrothermal systems sustained by ultramafic intrusions (Cathles et al., 1997), and characterization of the exchange between hydrothermal fluids and magmatic intrusions (Norton & Taylor, 1979). Convective heat extraction as it relates to geothermal wells, economic viability, temperatures near the liquidus, and different magma types has been investigated, but not the timescales of said heat extraction (Hardee, 1981; Hardee & Dunn, 1981; Hardee & Larson, 1977). 

The mechanisms for heat extraction from a magma body that could contribute to crystallization of these large (>50 km<sup>3</sup> ) magma bodies on the order of decades to centuries are not well understood. In this study, we compare the effects of conductive heat transfer—through contact of the magma body with the overlying crust—and convective heat transfer—caused by the movement of heat by the flow of fluids in the crust overlying the magma body (Boden, 2016). We follow Gualda, Pamukcu, et al. (2012), Pamukcu, Gualda, Ghiorso, et al. (2015), and Gualda et al. (2018) in using rhyolite-MELTS to model the change in enthalpy (total heat loss) during crystallization of a magma body at constant pressure. Then, we compare heat transfer within the overlying crust by various conductive and convective heat flow models to calculate the time required to lose the total heat obtained from rhyolite-MELTS. To test the sensitivity of our results to variability inherent in the model parameters, we used a Monte Carlo approach. 

We constrain our model by using geological information from the Mamaku ignimbrite, the product of the ca. 240 ka Rotorua caldera-forming eruption in the central TVZ (Bégué et al., 2014; Gravley et al., 2007). This region is an ideal place for this research, since it is home to hydrothermal systems sitting within and on the margins of large silicic calderas (Bibby et al., 1995; Bégué et al., 2014; Wilson & Rowland, 2016). There is also a wealth of knowledge regarding geothermal systems due to geothermal energy exploration and production in the region (Bibby et al., 1995; Hedenquist, 1990; Rowland & Simmons, 2012), which has provided us with an understanding of the cross-sectional fluid and rock properties, as well as the reservoir conditions of these dynamic systems. By characterizing the influence of hydrothermal systems on magma bodies and providing a link between crustal processes and magmatic processes, we expand the toolkit for those who monitor and assess the hazards associated with restless calderas. 

### **1.1. Geological Background** 

The central TVZ includes deposits of many caldera-forming eruptions, having erupted ∼6,000 km<sup>3</sup> of rhyolitic material over a period of ∼1.6 Ma (Houghton et al., 1995; Wilson et al., 1995). The TVZ flare up (Gravley et al., 2016) was a time of highly productive rhyolitic volcanism that lasted from ∼350 to 240 ka, with three main pulses that led to a total of ∼3,000 km<sup>3</sup> of tephra erupted from multiple caldera-forming eruptions. 

The Mamaku ignimbrite erupted in the third pulse of the TVZ flare up from the Rotorua Caldera (Figure 1) at ca. 240 ka (Gravley et al., 2007); it has a volume of >145 km<sup>3</sup> and was sourced from multiple magma batches extracted from the same crystal mush (Bégué et al., 2014; Gravley et al., 2016). The Mamaku ignimbrite was chosen for this study because the Rotorua caldera currently hosts a geothermal system, and Ti-in-quartz geochronometry of pumice-hosted quartz in the Mamaku ignimbrite shows crystallization on the order of decades to centuries (Gualda et al., 2018). Despite recent disputes about Ti-in-quartz timescales (Gualda & Pamukçu, 2020; Jollands et al., 2020a, 2020b), crystallization timescales are confirmed by other

### **2.2. Current Surface Heat Flux in the TVZ** 

Previous estimates of the overall heat budget of the TVZ (Hochstein, 1995) show that the present-day heat output is between 3,000 and 4,000 MW across the entire rift, which is approximately 200 km long and 25 km wide. With these values, the rift-average heat flux is between 0.6 and 0.8 W/m<sup>2</sup> . Alternative estimates (Dempsey, 2012; Weir, 2009) take into account the average area of a geothermal field, the average power of a geothermal field, and how many geothermal fields fit into the footprint of a magma body. The average heat flux through a geothermal field is calculated as 1.4 W/m<sup>2</sup> using these assumptions (Dempsey, 2012; Weir, 2009). Using 0.7 and 1.4 W/m<sup>2</sup> as the lower and upper bounds, respectively, for our calculations, subsequently referred to as qTVZ, calculated timescales of heat loss, tcool, are on the order of millennia, which are too slow compared to the crystallization timescales from quartz geochronometry from Gualda et al. (2018) (see above).

### **3.2. Convection** 

The inability of a pure conductive layer to lose enough heat on the timescales inferred from geochronometry indicates that the appropriate heat extraction mechanism likely incorporates a hydrothermal convective component. This is particularly likely in the case of the TVZ (but also many other volcanically active areas), where geothermal systems are ubiquitous and well developed (Bibby et al., 1995; Rowland & Simmons, 2012). In such a convective system, conduction occurs in a lower boundary layer, the viscoelastic shell, above the magma body until the boiling temperature of the hydrothermal fluids is reached at ∼300–350°C (Dempsey et al., 2018; Weir, 2009) (Figure 3b). Above this layer, advection of hot fluids leads to a region in which temperature is nearly constant and convective heat transfer is dominant. Close to the Earth's surface, temperature rapidly drops to a surface atmospheric value (Rowland & Simmons, 2012). Introducing fluid advection allows for heat extraction systems that are more efficient than conduction alone. 

Energy conservation requires that, in steady state, each of the layers described above (lower conductive layer, middle advective layer, and upper boundary layer) transfer heat at the same rate. For the lower conductive layer, temperature boundary conditions are well constrained, at ∼750°C in contact with the magma body (Gualda et al., 2018) and at 300–350°C at the BDT temperature of quartzo-feldspathic crust, after the onset of quartz plasticity at 300°C (Scholz, 1988), assumed to be the lower permeable limit accessible by hydrothermal fluids (Dempsey et al., 2018; Rowland & Simmons, 2012). There is also the work of Fournier (1999), in which similar temperatures of the BDT around 360 °C are estimated, where hydrostatic pressure is dominant and strain rates are in line with common values of actively deforming areas. However, Fournier (1999) also shows that times where strain rate increases contribute to a hotter BDT of 870°C, but these times of higher strain would occur during dome emplacement or collapse due to eruption, processes not associated with storage. In order to crystallize 10 wt% crystals in the Mamaku magma on decades to century timescales, the lower conductive layer would need to be ∼5.9–102 m thick. This is (mostly) an order of magnitude smaller than the estimate of Dempsey et al. (2018), who calculated a value of ∼450 m. Depending on the temperature at the brittle ductile transition, a minimum magma volume of ∼100 km<sup>3</sup> can be cooled over 100 years with a lower conductive layer on the order of 100 m thick. A conductive layer thickness approaching ∼450 m would be sufficient for magma of ∼22–25 km<sup>3</sup> . This implies that the magma system may likely be divided between different lenses that are smaller in volume than the modeled 150 km<sup>3</sup> , with smaller volumes cooling over shorter timescales than larger volumes that are cooled at the same rate.

## **4. Discussion** 

### **4.1. Conduction Versus Convection** 

The initial goal of this study was to pursue conductive and convective heat flow models to determine which mechanism is more likely to contribute to heat loss from a magma body on timescales on the order of decades to centuries. When comparing conductive and convective heat flow, the results indicate that decadal to centennial-scale heat loss cannot happen as a result of pure conductive heat flow over the entire crustal thickness above a large magma body, with all timescales for heat loss by conduction leading to crystallization times greater than 1,000 years. The heat flux of the rainfall convection model is much closer to qMELTS than heat flux from the constant conductive model (∼5 W/m<sup>2</sup> vs. 1.3 W/m<sup>2</sup> ). Therefore, a variant of a convective system characterized by conductive heat transfer in the crust around the magma body but dominated by convective heat transfer through hydrothermal fluids is more likely to be the mechanism by which heat was extracted from the magma body that led to the caldera-forming eruption of ∼150 km<sup>3</sup> of Mamaku magma, in order to be consistent with crystallization timescales derived from quartz crystals. We further explore—in addition to the rainfall convective model—a groundwater flow model, which is consistent with the convective scenar- 

io proposed above, but which allows us to better investigate some of the variations possible in convective heat loss. In an area like the TVZ, with ∼20 hydrothermal systems (Bibby et al., 1995; Rowland & Simmons, 2012), this heat extraction mechanism is likely to have an effect on relatively shallow silicic magma bodies cooling in the crust. 

Variations in water content and magma composition can lead to variations in the value of ΔHtotal. A particularly striking aspect of the behavior of convective heat loss timescales using the groundwater flow model is that there is persistence in the order of magnitude of decadal to centennial values for tcool (Figure 6). Even when crystallization in rhyolite-MELTS is extended until 20 wt% crystals, which leads to an increase in ΔHtotal, the majority of values of tcool calculated using flux from Equation 7 are centennial in magnitude (Figure 6c). 

In order to analyze the probability that calculated heat loss timescales from the groundwater-controlled hydrothermal model exceed 100 years, we show the distribution of the exceedance probabilities of heat loss times, tcool, whose enthalpy changes are calculated from quartz saturation qtz until 10 wt% crystals. Figure 7a demonstrates that 50% of timescales are less than 100 years, and 90% are less than 280 years. These timescales are in line with the evidence of short crystallization and storage on the order of decades to centuries from Gualda et al. (2018), drawing an important connection between a crystallizing magma body and the crustal processes that remove heat from it.

### **4.2. Coupling of Magmatic and Hydrothermal Heat Loss** 

A question that arises from this research is how the heat is distributed throughout the magma body. If it is assumed that the entire magma body is well-mixed, as we do, then, based on the maximum value of qTVZ of 1.4 W/m<sup>2</sup> (Dempsey, 2012; Weir, 2009), the thickness of a magma body that loses the required amount of heat in 100 years is ∼100 m, and the thickness of a magma body that loses the required heat in 10 years is ∼10 m. Both values give a magma thickness 1–2 orders of magnitude less than the assumed 1 km thickness, seemingly indicating that a wellmixed magma body of volume 150 km<sup>3</sup> could not sufficiently cool in the required time. However, the actual subsurface expression of the Mamaku magma body could be a patchwork of multiple smaller magma bodies (Bégué et al., 2014), with different magma lenses or different volumes that are not arranged in any particular way but are within the footprint of the caldera structure, rather than the simpler assumption of a single pancake-shaped magma body (G. F. Cooper et al., 2012; see also Cashman & Giordano, 2014; Gualda & Ghiorso, 2013b). This patchwork arrangement, where smaller magma bodies of sizes such as 30 km<sup>3</sup> could possibly allow for these smaller magma bodies to cool, erupt, and possibly trigger eruption of other magma bodies in the system. This could resolve the disagreement between our calculations of the size of the lower conductive viscoelastic shell above the magma body and the calculations of Dempsey et al. (2018). These magma bodies could be spread out over an area greater than 150 km<sup>3</sup> , which would increase total heat output if constant heat flux is assumed; or they could span a thickness greater than 1 km, which could impact the depth extent incorporated into our calculations. If the magma is believed to be one contiguous body, the effect of transient permeability on decreasing the size of the viscoelastic shell could be investigated (Mittal & Richards, 2019). 

### **4.3. Is There a Mamaku-Sized Caldera-Forming Magma Body Beneath Rotorua Now?** 

Comparison of heat flow timescales to quartz crystallization timescales shows that hydrothermal systems are the only mechanism able to remove heat from a crystallizing magma body at sufficiently fast rates. We now explore more closely the effect of Monte Carlo analysis of the groundwater heat flow model on the calculated heat output, Q, using values calculated from the current TVZ as constraints for our analysis. Figure 7b shows the distribution of heat output calculated as a result of the Monte Carlo analysis of the groundwater hydrothermal model that yield heat loss timescales of 100 years or less. The mean and median heat output values are ∼1,000 MW. When looking at the distribution in Figure 7b, the values for heat output range from ∼400 to 1,600 MW, or 1 to 4 times the heat output from the current Rotorua hydrothermal system. However, the majority of power output values are 2–3 times the size of the current Rotorua system (470 MW) (Bibby et al., 1995), which indicates it is not very likely that a Mamaku-sized magma body is currently present in the crust and cooling on the order of decades below the Rotorua caldera. It is likely that larger heat output values could be typical at times when a large, silicic magma body is being stored in the crust.

### **4.4. Implications for the Current TVZ and Beyond** 

While heat flow due to hydrothermal convection appears to be essential for decadal heat loss timescales from a magma body the size of that inferred for eruption and deposition of the Mamaku ignimbrite, it is important to note that the presence of hydrothermal systems of that magnitude may not mean that there is a large, eruptible, silicic magma body being stored in the shallow crust. Currently, there are areas within the TVZ that exhibit heat output similar to the mean heat output from the groundwater heat flow model displayed in Figure 7b. The Waimangu, Waiotapu, and Reporoa hydrothermal systems, which are located roughly 20 km southeast of the current Rotorua system, together have a heat output of ∼1,000 MW over an area of ∼110 km<sup>2</sup> (Bibby et al., 1994, 1995; Rowland & Simmons, 2012). Rowland and Simmons (2012) indicate that heat flow in the TVZ is relatively stable despite frequent volcanic eruptions. They also champion the idea of a “hot zone” in the lower to middle crust, where basaltic magmas intrude and contribute to the generation and eruption of silicic mush that drives the present-day heat flow in the region (Rowland & Simmons, 2012). Therefore, heat flow values similar to those inferred to have existed above the Mamaku magma body just prior to eruption do not necessarily indicate shallow magma storage. 

An understanding of when a large, silicic magma body is being stored in the crust is essential for distinguishing between relatively benign hydrothermal activity and activity that indicates the possibility of a large, caldera-forming eruption. Whether that be through seismicity, ground deformation, anomalous gas signatures in hydrothermal systems that indicate new magma, possible elevated heat flow measurements, or the generation of new hydrothermal systems, accumulation of a magma body of ∼150 km<sup>3</sup> will likely bring about change that can be viewed at the surface. If we can characterize and quantify these changes in the hydrothermal system, we may have the potential to detect a large volume of silicic magma being stored in the shallow crust and gauge its eruptibility with respect to time. 

## **5. Conclusion** 

We have compared the heat loss timescales of a magma body under the influence of conductive and convective heat flow models. Convective models that are governed by hydrothermal systems give heat loss timescales closest to crystallization times from quartz geochronometry, with a heat output on the order of 1,000 MW required for removing heat from a large, silicic magma body on the order of decades to centuries. This ephemeral nature of the Mamaku magma body holds regardless of storage pressure, water saturation,

The question of how heat is lost throughout the magma body brings up the possibility of multiple magma bodies of smaller size crystallizing on the order of decades, rather than a single well-mixed magma body 150 km<sup>3</sup> in volume. This can resolve discrepancies between thicknesses of the viscoelastic shell around the magma body, as well as decrease the heat output required of a hydrothermal system to lose heat on the order of decades. Future studies could explore this relationship further by coupling magma bodies and hydrothermal systems in more detail. 

The current heat output of the Rotorua hydrothermal system is 2–3 times smaller than the majority of power outputs calculated using the groundwater heat flow model that used the current variability in the modeling parameters in the TVZ, so the presence of a Mamaku-sized magma body cooling on the order of decades below Rotorua at present is not likely. There are three closely spaced hydrothermal bodies in the TVZ that collectively have a heat output on the order of 1,000 MW, but the driver of these systems is thought to be from a deeper zone of the crust than the depth of a shallowly stored silicic magma in the TVZ. Thus, the challenge is knowing when such large silicic magma bodies are being stored in the shallow crust so that we can apply our increasing understanding of their eruptible timescales. Through knowledge of distribution of magma bodies as single entities or a patchwork of bodies in the crust as well as when silicic magma is collecting in the crust, future studies could utilize the results of this investigation in relation to cooling of magma bodies of different size under different heat flow regimes in order to improve the ways we monitor restless calderas in New Zealand and elsewhere.

### **6.2. Total Enthalpy Loss Modeling** 

In order to model the total heat loss of the Mamaku magmatic system over its cooling and crystallization history, we used rhyolite-MELTS, a set of thermodynamic models developed to model fluid-bearing silicic magma systems (Gualda & Ghiorso, 2014; Gualda, Ghiorso, et al., 2012; see also Asimow & Ghiorso, 1998; Ghiorso & Sack, 1995). Enthalpy loss as a function of crystallization is assumed to be a proxy for crystallization time of the magma body. Heat loss is assumed to be in a steady state, causing enthalpy to be correlated with time by a constant heat loss rate that is spatially averaged over the surface expression of the Mamaku magma body. Rhyolite-MELTS is used to calculate isobaric cooling sequences, which yield a complete description of the crystallization path (phases present, their compositions, thermodynamic properties of phases and system, etc.) for a given system composition for a set temperature interval, at a specified pressure. Whole-rock compositions of pumice are used because pumice is believed to be an erupted parcel of magma, recording the composition of the system prior to crystallization. 

To explore the effect of pressure on the total enthalpy loss, separate isobaric cooling sequences are calculated using the average Mamaku pumice composition of Gualda et al. (2018), at pressures from 50 to 150 MPa in 25 MPa pressure steps. The magma is assumed to be initially fluid saturated. Initial values for FeO and Fe2O3 were determined using rhyolite-MELTS from measured total FeO values by assuming _fO2_ equal to that of the Ni-NiO buffer. Calculations are allowed to continue until a maximum of 10 wt% crystals is reached, a value constrained by the total crystal content of typical Mamaku pumice. The amount of crystals in rhyolite-MELTS is determined by dividing the mass of the crystal phases by the mass of the system (excluding exsolved water). Separate isobaric cooling sequences are also calculated for the whole-rock compositions shown in Table 1, using the pressure retrieved from rhyolite-MELTS geobarometry of matrix glass (see Table 2). For each composition, sequences are calculated from initial conditions with water fractions of 0.1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 relative to saturation to allow for variability and uncertainty in the initial saturation conditions of the magma. Calculations are also allowed to continue until a threshold value of 10 wt% crystals is reached. The enthalpy values and mass calculated during the isobaric cooling sequences are used to calculate the total change in heat as a result of crystallization, ΔHtotal. 

### **6.3. Monte Carlo Modeling for Groundwater Flow** 

Uncertainty in magma storage pressures comes from uncertainty in the compositions of Mamaku glass compositions. In order to capture this, 300 compositions were generated from a Gaussian distribution of one of the Mamaku glass compositions from a prescribed standard deviation and mean, shown in Table 2. GL2306-G was chosen as the mean, and it has a corresponding standard deviation calculated from the multiple analyses used to calculate the mean composition. Each of these compositions was used as input for the rhyolite-MELTS geobarometer (Gualda & Ghiorso, 2014), 298 of which returned results, giving 298 storage pressures, of which the mean and standard deviation were calculated and used to generate a Gaussian distribution. The distribution of storage pressures was used to calculate a distribution of storage depths used in the Monte Carlo analysis of the Peclet number.
