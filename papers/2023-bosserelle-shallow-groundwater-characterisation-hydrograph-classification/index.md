## The problem

Groundwater lies at shallow depth beneath many low-lying coastal cities.
Sea-level rise can raise this groundwater (Habel et al., 2023; Knott et al.,
2019). Rising groundwater can damage pipes and basements below the ground
surface and roads and buildings above it (Befus et al., 2020; May, 2020; Su et
al., 2022; Thorndahl et al., 2016).

Christchurch is a coastal city of more than 400,000 people, built on flat,
low-lying sediments close to rivers and the sea. Following White et al.
(2007), vegetation recorded on maps compiled in 1856 suggests that wetland or
near-surface groundwater covered 5,215 ha of the Avon-Heathcote area before
the city developed. That is about 52 km², an area roughly 30% the size of the
173 km² that we mapped (our arithmetic). By 2015 to 2016, less than 6% of that
wetland remained. Our goal in this study was to characterise this shallow
groundwater, including how its depth varies in space and time and how it
relates to natural and human stressors.

## What we did

Most of our records came from the Automatic Piezometer Project network. The
Christchurch City Council acquired it from the Earthquake Commission in 2016,
after the 2010–2011 Canterbury Earthquake Sequence (CES). Its 247 sites have
loggers that record the water table every 10 minutes. We added long-term
manual wells and regional council sites, and selected sites with records from
2016 to 2020. Because land subsided during the earthquakes, ground elevation
models are uncertain. We therefore worked with depth to groundwater, which is
likely to be more accurate than a water-table elevation.

First, we interpolated median depths between wells onto a 200 m grid. We then
used Moran's I, a statistic of spatial similarity, to check whether the size
of water-level fluctuations was grouped in space. We tested each record for a
trend with the seasonal Kendall test, which allows for seasonal variation.
Next, we sorted 210 records, each 39.5 months long and sampled every 4 hours,
into groups of similar shape with k-means clustering. Finally, we used
cross-correlation to measure how closely groundwater levels matched ocean
levels at the Sumner tide gauge over 25 to 31 December 2019.

## What we found

Across 265 sites, the mean depth to groundwater was 1.39 m. The median depth
was 1 m or less below about 62% of the interpolated area, 1 to 2 m below 35%,
and more than 2 m below only 3%. At 91% of sites, the standard deviation of
depth (a measure of the size of water-level fluctuations) was 0.3 m or less.

From the coast, along the estuary and inland to Ensors Road bridge on the
Heathcote River and Fitzgerald Avenue bridge on the Avon River, the median
water table lies below mean high water springs (MHWS). MHWS is the average
high tide during spring tides, the tides with the largest range. This part of
the eastern suburbs is likely to experience shoaling (a rise of the water
table towards the surface) and flooding as the sea rises.

Neighbouring sites tended to have similar fluctuation sizes. Small
fluctuations clustered north of the central city, in Fendalton, Merivale, St
Albans and Edgeware, and near Travis Wetland. Large fluctuations clustered in
Avonside, Richmond and Hoon Hay. Of the sites with the smallest fluctuations,
80% had a median depth within 1.5 m of the ground.

From 2016 to 2020, the water table was probably rising at 40% of the 245
automatic sites and probably falling at 46%, with no clear trend at the rest.
Long-term manual wells showed 32% rising and 50% falling before the CES. The
recent falls are consistent with a gradual decrease in rainfall. However, the
rises did not follow rainfall. Their cause is unexplained, but one possible
cause is the sealing of sewer networks (Becker et al., 2022).

Clustering sorted the 210 records into groups of 15, 43, 106 and 46. The group
of 15 had flashy records, with frequent, sharp peaks, from sites concentrated
in a small area north-west of the central city, all within 200 m of surface
water. The other three groups had no clear relation to depth, fluctuation size
or distance from surface water. Clustering successfully classified responses
to rainfall and stream flow, but dewatering (pumping out groundwater) was
harder to pick out because its signal overlaps with tides and rainfall. The
similar shapes of most records hint at the potential to move some loggers to
new locations, from sites whose trends and statistics resemble others and that
lie outside the shallowest areas.

The closer a site was to a tidal river, the more closely its groundwater level
followed the ocean tide. The ocean seemed to influence shallow groundwater
mainly through the estuary and tidal rivers. These preliminary results come
from one week of records.

## Limitations

Monitoring has several large gaps along the coast and near the estuary. We
also assumed a depth of 0 m at the coast. As a result, the interpolation
placed the water table at 0.7 and 0.9 m near the Heathcote River mouth and in
South New Brighton, against measured medians of 1.5 and 2.4 m. Over less than
4 years of records, our methods did not detect interactions between
groundwater and underground infrastructure. Numerical modelling may represent
these interactions better (Attard et al., 2016a, b). We recommend continued
monitoring with continuous sensors, with priority near the tidal rivers, the
estuary and the coast, so that changes in groundwater under a rising sea can
be detected.

## Glossary

**Groundwater** is water held in the pores of soil and sediment below the
ground surface.

The **water table** is the top of the groundwater, where the sediment becomes
saturated.

**Depth to groundwater** is the distance from the ground surface down to the
water table, here in metres below ground level.

The **Canterbury Earthquake Sequence (CES)** is the series of earthquakes that
struck Christchurch in 2010 and 2011.

A **hectare (ha)** is 10,000 m², and 100 ha make 1 km².

**Interpolation** estimates values between measurement points to make a
continuous map.

**Standard deviation** measures how far a record varies about its average.

**Moran's I** is a statistic that measures whether values at nearby sites are
more similar than values at distant sites.

The **seasonal Kendall test** detects a trend in a time series while allowing
for regular seasonal variation.

**K-means clustering** is a machine-learning method that sorts records into a
chosen number of groups by similarity.

**Cross-correlation** measures how closely two time series match, allowing one
to lag behind the other.

**Mean high water springs (MHWS)** is the average height of high tide during
spring tides, 1.368 m in the New Zealand Vertical Datum 2016.

**Spring tides** are the tides with the largest range between high and low
water.

**Shoaling** groundwater is groundwater whose level is rising towards the
ground surface.

A **flashy** record rises and falls quickly, with frequent, sharp peaks.

**Dewatering** is the removal of groundwater by pumping or drainage.

## Abstract

Groundwater is present at shallow depth under many coastal low-lying cities. Despite the importance of protecting coastal urbanised areas from flooding and climate-change-induced sea-level rise, the effects of shallow groundwater fluctuations are rarely investigated. The aim of this study was to determine characteristics of shallow groundwater, including spatial and temporal trends in depths to groundwater and their relationship to natural and anthropogenic stressors. The study uses depth to groundwater measurements from a uniquely extensive and densely spaced monitoring network in Ōtautahi/Christchurch, New Zealand. Data-driven analysis approaches were applied, including spatial interpolation, autocorrelation, clustering, cross-correlation and trend analysis. These approaches are not commonly applied for groundwater assessments despite the potential for them to provide insights and information for city-wide systems. The comprehensive approach revealed discernible clusters and trends within the dataset. Responses to stresses such as rainfall events and stream flow were successfully classified using clustering analysis. The time series analysis indicated that in areas of shallow groundwater, low variation in levels occurred and this was also found using clustering. However, attributing some clusters to specific hydrogeological attributes or stressors posed challenges. The primary feature in hydrograph classification proved to be the proximity to tidal rivers and their correlation with tidal signals. These results highlight the value of using large datasets to characterise spatial and temporal variability of shallow groundwater in urban coastal settings and to assist with monitoring infrastructure planning in the face of future climate-change hazards.
